## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided investigation applies [Week 3’s lesson](/lectures/week-03/).
Allow about 30 minutes, plus 10 more for the partner variant in step 7.
Everything you need is on this page. You need something
to write with and nothing else: no lock, no tools, no paid book, no AI account.
Priya is fictional.

You are examining evidence of understanding, not sitting a test about locks. You
will not learn to open one, and nothing here is instruction in picking.

Work alone, or with a partner using the variant in step 7. The reference below is
hidden behind a toggle on purpose: opening it is a step in the procedure, and the
procedure asks you to close it again.

If you did the slide exercises, reuse those answers and start at step 4.

## 1. Declare what you already know — 1 minute

Write one line: what did you already know about how a key lock works, and where
from? A locksmith, a disassembled padlock, a video, nothing at all.

Record it before you read anything. Prior familiarity changes what a
reconstruction demonstrates, and a line written afterwards is hard to trust —
including by you.

## 2. Study the reference — 4 minutes

Open the toggle, read it once or twice, then close it and leave it closed until
step 5 says otherwise.

<details>
<summary>The reference: how a pin tumbler lock works</summary>

An outer **casing** has a cylindrical hole in it. Inside that hole sits a
rotating cylinder called the **plug**. To unlock the door, the plug has to turn.
The front face of the plug carries a straight slot, the **keyway**, which the key
enters. At the back of the plug is a **cam**: a projecting piece that, as the
plug turns, draws back the **bolt**, the metal bar holding the door shut.

Five or six holes are drilled down through the plug. Each holds a **key pin**,
cut to its own length and rounded underneath so a key can slide across it.
Directly above each key pin, in a shaft in the casing, sits a **driver pin**,
with a **spring** above it pressing down. A key pin and the driver pin above it
make up one **stack**. The boundary where the plug meets the casing is the
**shear line**: the surface along which the plug has to rotate.

With **no key**, the springs push each stack down, so every driver pin is forced
part-way out of the casing and into the plug. Pins cross the boundary, and the
plug cannot turn.

With the **correct key**, its cut edge lifts each key pin by a different, exact
amount, so every key-pin/driver-pin junction comes level with the shear line.
Nothing crosses the boundary, the plug turns, and the cam draws the bolt back.

With a **wrong key**, the cuts lift the stacks by the wrong amounts. Lifted too
little, a driver pin still crosses into the plug; lifted too far, a key pin is
pushed up into the casing. One wrong stack is enough to hold the plug.

Technical grounding: [Wikipedia’s article on the pin tumbler lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock),
which names the boundary the *shear point*. That article grounds the mechanism
and is not evidence for any claim about understanding.

</details>

## 3. Reconstruct it — 8 minutes

With the reference closed, write out how a pin tumbler lock works in your own
words. Name the parts. Say where each one sits relative to the others. Explain
why the plug cannot turn with no key, why it turns with the correct key, and
what a wrong key does.

Preserve this text exactly as you first write it. Everything after this step
works on it, and a tidied version destroys the record.

**About the closed toggle.** You can reopen it, and nobody is watching. A source
removal you administer to yourself is not something another person can check.
Follow the instruction anyway for a practical reason: a copied version leaves
step 5 nothing to examine, so you lose the exercise rather than fail a test. If
you do look, write *“reconstructed with one look at the reference”* in your
record. A recorded look is usable evidence; an unrecorded one is what ruins the
page.

## 4. Note what it felt like — 1 minute

Before you check anything, write one line: which part of the mechanism felt
least secure while you were writing?

Keep this separate from the annotation in step 5. It is a **self-report** about
the experience of writing, not evidence about the text. Comparing the two later
is interesting precisely because they often disagree.

## 5. Restore the source and annotate — 5 minutes

Reopen the toggle in step 2 and read it beside your own version. **Do not rewrite
your text.** Mark each difference in the margin with one letter:

| Letter | What it marks | Example of the loss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **P** | A **part** omitted altogether. | No springs anywhere in your text. |
| **R** | A **relation** omitted: parts named but not placed. | Driver pins named, but not said to sit above the key pins in the casing. |
| **C** | A **causal step** omitted: a “because” the mechanism needs. | The plug turns with the right key, but nothing says why a clear boundary allows it. |
| **X** | A **scenario** omitted: a case the reference covered. | No account of over-lifting by a wrong key. |

Then count them: how many P, R, C and X.

<details>
<summary>Compare with a fictional annotated reconstruction</summary>

Priya wrote:

> “A door lock has a barrel that turns when you put the right key in. Inside
> there are pins of different heights. The key pushes the pins up to the right
> heights, and then the barrel can turn and the door opens. A wrong key does not
> push them to the right heights, so it will not turn.”

Nothing in it is false. Every sentence is consistent with the reference, which is
exactly why counting matters more than judging it by feel.

*P* — four parts gone: springs; driver pins, since she has one set of pins where
the reference had two; the cam; the bolt. *R* — a barrel with pins “inside,” but no casing and
nothing about driver pins sitting above key pins. *C* — the shear line is gone,
so “right heights, so it turns” states a rule with no mechanism under it. *X* —
the wrong key appears, but only as under-lifting.

Counts: P 4, R 2, C 1, X 1.

If your own text contained most of the reference, annotate it that way and say
so. A strong performance is a result, not a problem with the exercise.

</details>

## 6. Weigh the annotation — 4 minutes

A count is not yet a judgement. Write two or three sentences deciding which of
your marks matter and why.

<details>
<summary>How the four types differ as evidence</summary>

A missing **P** is often a vocabulary gap. You may hold the arrangement and lack
the phrase “driver pin,” and describing the same arrangement in your own words
would show it.

A missing **C** is the strongest finding of the four. Without the causal step
there is no account of why the outcome follows, so the text could have been
produced by someone who never took in the boundary at all. Priya can say what
happens; she cannot yet say what makes it happen. A lost noun and a lost
“because” are not the same loss.

A missing **R** sits between them: the parts are there, so something was
retained, but an unplaced part does no work in the mechanism.

A missing **X** is the least conclusive. You may have judged the wrong-key case
not worth writing rather than not having it. Where it matters, ask for the
scenario directly instead of inferring from its absence.

</details>

## 7. Optional: make the removal witnessed — 10 minutes

**With a partner.** Give them this page. They read the reference aloud once, then
keep it and ask you to reconstruct it while they watch. They annotate your text
against the reference and report the counts.

This fixes one specific defect: the closure of the source becomes an observation
somebody else can report, rather than your own account of it. That is a genuine
improvement in the condition, and it is the only thing it improves.

**Working alone**, you lose exactly that. Your record still stands, but the line
“reference closed” is self-report, and self-report is not independent evidence of
how an answer was produced. Write the condition as *self-administered and
unwitnessed* and claim less from the result.

A partner adds observation of the conditions. It does not give either of you
access to how the text was actually produced — whether it was rebuilt in the
moment or remembered whole from four minutes earlier.

## 8. Keep the record — 4 minutes

Preserve this with your annotated page. Week 4 asks you to compare against it.

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Prior familiarity | Your line from step 1, unrevised. |
| Conditions | Reference closed or looked at; alone or witnessed; written or spoken; time taken. |
| Original text | Your reconstruction, preserved before annotation. |
| Counts | P, R, C, X. |
| Felt gap | Your line from step 4, and whether the annotation agreed with it. |
| Observation | What your text actually contained. |
| Interpretation | The specific thing it shows you can produce. |
| Alternative | Could verbatim memory, a look back, or prior familiarity explain it? |
| Limit | The claim that would still need more evidence. |

## 9. Defend one judgement — 3 minutes

Finish by writing two sentences about your own page: one claim the annotation
supports, and one it does not. Name the alternative explanation you cannot rule
out for each.

For example: “With the reference closed on my own account, I produced all three
states with the shear line doing the causal work. That supports the claim that I
can rebuild this mechanism’s structure minutes after reading it. It does not show
that I built the structure rather than remembered the passage, because nobody
observed the closure and I had read it four minutes earlier.”

If your reconstruction was complete, take that seriously rather than looking for
something to have gone wrong. Say what it establishes — a structure produced with
the source out of view, at that moment — and then say that it does not establish
that the structure was built rather than memorised, that the source was truly
closed, or that the same would happen for a mechanism you had not just read.

Do not conclude that reconstruction is the better way to read. It is a measuring
instrument, and you could not have reconstructed anything you had never consumed.
Week 4 takes this same lock and changes the conditions around it, which is the
only way to find out how much of today’s result belonged to the task rather than
to you.
