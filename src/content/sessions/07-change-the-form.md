## Where Week 6 left us

Week 6 changed the resources available during a performance. This week keeps attention on the idea but changes its form: audience, representation or problem. It tests a different ladder assumption: that an Explanation-rung response reflects an adaptable model rather than a rehearsed form. Our new fictional example supplies the background it needs.

## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided activity applies [Week 7's lesson](/lectures/week-07/).
Allow 25 minutes. All required information is below. Work alone; a solo
version is given for the partner step. No specialist knowledge, paid tool or
AI account is required. Kavi and the loan example from the lecture are
fictional teaching material, not data about you.

Choose one concept you already feel reasonably confident about, from any
subject — a hobby, a subject you study, something you learned recently for
its own sake. Write its name down before you start; you will return to the
same concept in every step.

## 1. First form: explain it plainly — 6 minutes

Write a short explanation of your chosen concept, as you would give it to
someone with no background in the topic at all. Aim for four to six
sentences. Use an analogy if one helps, but make sure the explanation would
let a genuine newcomer follow the idea, not just recognise that you know
words associated with it.

Keep this explanation; you will compare it against your second attempt.

## 2. Second form: a harder question — 8 minutes

Now write a second, separate performance of the *same* underlying idea, but
in a form your first explanation does not cover. Pick whichever of these
changes the form the most for your concept:

- **Change the audience:** explain it to someone who already knows the
  general area and would ask a sharper, more technical question.
- **Change the representation:** turn your explanation into a worked example,
  a comparison of two cases, or a diagram description, instead of prose.
- **Change the problem:** use the idea to answer a question you did not
  address the first time — apply it, defend it against an objection, or
  compare it with a near neighbour that is often confused with it.

Write three to six sentences or a short worked example. Do this before
reading further; comparing too early defeats the purpose.

## 3. Compare the two performances — 6 minutes

Answer these questions in writing, using both performances you just wrote.

<details>
<summary>Compare after attempting both performances</summary>

There is no single correct pair of answers here — the check is whether your
second performance actually did new work. Ask yourself:

- Did the second performance restate the first one in different words, or
  did it use the underlying relationship to do something the first
  performance never had to do?
- Could someone who only memorised your first explanation, without
  understanding the mechanism behind it, have produced your second
  performance too? If yes, the change of form has not yet told you anything
  new.
- Is there a specific claim in the second performance that only follows if
  the underlying idea, not just its surface description, is correctly held?
  Name it.

Kavi's lecture example gives a worked case: the sibling explanation ("interest
earns interest, like a snowball") was fluent but reused verbatim by a version
of Kavi that then reasoned incorrectly about a new comparison. A version that
instead used the mechanism — more compounding events raise the total, not
smaller individual amounts — reached the right answer for the right reason.
The difference between the two was visible only once the form changed.

</details>

## 4. What the comparison does and does not establish — 3 minutes

Write two sentences: one naming what your two performances together support
about your own understanding of the concept, and one naming what they still
do not establish. If your second performance was itself something you had
produced before in a similar shape — a comparison you had already thought
through, an audience you regularly explain this to — say so; a form that is
new to a stranger is not necessarily new to you, and that changes what the
pair is evidence of.

## 5. Name the next form — 2 minutes

Barnett and Ceci's taxonomy for transfer distinguishes several ways a new
context can differ from the original: domain, audience, representation, and
several others. Name one dimension your two performances did *not* vary, and
describe briefly what a third performance, changed along that dimension,
would need to show that the first two do not.

For the source behind this week's distinction, read Barnett and Ceci (2002),
["When and Where Do We Apply What We Learn? A Taxonomy for Far
Transfer"](https://rapunselshair.pbworks.com/f/barnett_2002.pdf), and ask
which of their nine dimensions your own pair of performances actually
changed.

## Working alone or with a partner

Working alone, write both performances yourself and use the questions above
as your own check — you lose an independent reader's judgement of whether the
second performance was genuinely different, but you can still be honest about
whether you reused the first explanation's structure.

Working with a partner, have them read only your first performance, then set
you the harder question or new representation themselves, without telling you
in advance what it will be. This is closer to Kavi's colleague and gives a
form neither of you pre-arranged — record whether this changed how confident
you are in your own answer compared with the solo version.
