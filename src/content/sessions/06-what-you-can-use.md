## Where Week 5 left us

Week 5 built a seven-rung ladder, but Recall and Reconstruction assume that removing a source makes the evidence stronger. This week tests that assumption by varying permitted resources. We move from the lock to a fictional courier; the examination question continues even though the setting changes.

## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided activity applies [Week 6's lesson](/lectures/week-06/). Allow
20 minutes. All required information is below. Work alone or with a partner; no
navigation app, live delivery, paid tool or AI account is required. The courier
case is fictional. You are examining what a resource-supported performance is
evidence of, not running a technical certification.

If you already work with one of the resources described below professionally,
record that familiarity rather than pretending the material is new.

## 1. Recap the four conditions — 2 minutes

Clark and Chalmers (1998), §4, give four conditions under which an external
resource functions like part of someone's own memory rather than an outside
reference: it is a **constant** presence consulted whenever relevant; the
information is **available without difficulty**; it is **automatically
endorsed** on retrieval; and it is there because of the person's own **past
endorsement** — they put it there. They treat the fourth as more arguable than
the first three.

## 2. Sort three cases — 5 minutes

For each case, decide whether the resource looks closer to an integrated part
of the person's own competence, or closer to a stranger's advice consulted for
the first time. Write one sentence of reasoning per case, naming which
condition(s) it passes or fails.

1. A hiker checks a trail map app for the first time, at a junction, on a
   trail they have never walked before.
2. A long-haul truck driver checks a route-planning app used on every shift
   for years, trusted without re-verifying, configured with her own preferred
   settings.
3. A student pastes a homework question into an AI tool opened minutes
   earlier and copies out whatever it returns without reading it.

<details>
<summary>Compare after sorting</summary>

The hiker's map fails constancy and past endorsement: first use, not assembled
from prior choices. The truck driver's app meets the strong conditions:
constant, effortless, trusted from repeated correct use, self-configured. The
student's AI use fails constancy and past endorsement in the same way as the
hiker, and additionally fails automatic endorsement in the way that matters —
the output was never checked before being copied, so what happened was not
trust earned by reliability but an absence of checking at all.

</details>

## 3. Design a content-change test — 8 minutes

Pick a resource you actually use for something familiar: a recipe app, a
calculator, a reference chart, a translation tool, a spellchecker, a GPS.

Write:

1. The task, and why the resource ordinarily meets most of the four
   conditions for you.
2. One change to the resource's **content** — not whether it is present —
   that would create a conflict between what the resource says and what a
   person actually engaging with the task would notice.
3. What a correct response to your changed version would demonstrate, in one
   sentence.
4. What it would still not demonstrate, in one sentence.

Preserve your original design before reading the comparison.

<details>
<summary>Compare your design with an example</summary>

Resource: a spellchecker used while writing in your first language, checked
constantly, trusted, self-configured — meets the four conditions well.
Content change: insert a correctly spelled word used in the wrong sense (e.g.
"there" for "their") that a spellchecker will not flag. A person editing for
meaning, not just accepting the checker's silence, catches it; a person who
treats "no red underline" as "no error" does not. This demonstrates whether
the writer is reading for sense rather than trusting the tool's silence as
proof of correctness. It does not demonstrate that they could write the
passage from nothing, with no draft to edit at all — a different task would be
needed for that.

</details>

## 4. Compare a presence test against a content test — 3 minutes

Write two short task descriptions for the same skill: one that tests by
removing the resource entirely, and one that tests by changing the resource's
content while keeping it present. Name one thing each version risks
mismeasuring.

For example: removing a translation app entirely from someone learning a new
language risks testing memorised vocabulary from a single recent lesson,
which not everyone would have had reason to memorise yet. Leaving the app
present but feeding it a sentence with a false friend or an idiom it
mistranslates risks nothing comparable — a correct catch shows engagement with
meaning regardless of how new the vocabulary is.

## 5. Keep a short record — 2 minutes

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Resource | What it is, and how habitual is it for you or your example person? |
| Fit against the four conditions | Which are met, which are not? |
| Version used | Presence/absence, or content change? |
| What it supports | The specific claim the response backs up. |
| Limit | What further claim would still need a different task. |

Working alone, you cannot independently verify your own account of how you
produced an answer. Working with a partner adds observation, not access to
their thoughts. Neither arrangement makes the conclusion certain.

For the underlying argument, read Clark and Chalmers (1998),
["The Extended Mind," §§4–5](https://www.consc.net/papers/extended.html). Ask
which condition is doing the most work in your own sorted cases.
