## What makes the inference defensible?

**Validity** concerns how well the evidence supports the particular interpretation and use we propose. A successful performance does not validate every claim about the person. This returns to Week 1’s distinction between an observation and an inference. Farhan is a new fictional hard case; compare it with your own Week 11 record, using the supplied fallback if needed.

## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided activity applies [Week 12's lesson](/lectures/week-12/).
Allow 20 minutes. Work alone; a partner variant is offered in step 4. No
specialist topic, paid tool or AI account is required. Farhan and the
overheard conversation in the lecture are fictional, built to stress-test the
course's own ladder; nothing here reports a real exam or a real case.

This activity does not ask you to build anything new. It asks you to look
honestly at evidence you already have, from your own work in this course, and
say exactly what it is and is not entitled to support.

## 1. Choose your evidence — 3 minutes

Pick one real record from earlier in this course: your Week 11 examination
(administered or received), a Week 5 or Week 9 record, a Week 2 or Week 8
transcript, or any performance of yours that still exists in writing.

If you did not keep a record from an earlier week, use a fresh short one
instead: explain something you know well, with a friend or on your own,
without notes, and write down what you said.

## 2. Write two sentences — 5 minutes

Write one sentence claiming what your chosen evidence justifies. Write a
second sentence stating, specifically, what it does not.

Match the shape from the lecture's worked response: name the exact condition
the evidence was produced under in the first sentence, and name the exact
thing that condition cannot rule out in the second. "I'm not completely
sure, but I think I understand it" does not meet this bar — it does not say
what is uncertain or why.

<details>
<summary>Compare after attempting your own two sentences</summary>

"My Week 11 examination shows that, under live questioning with sources
removed, I could explain [topic] and respond to one unforeseen objection. It
does not show that I could do this on a topic I had no chance to prepare
for, or that my Challenge-rung response was not partly something I had
already rehearsed for a different reason."

If your two sentences could apply equally well to almost any evidence you
might have chosen, they are too generic. A useful second sentence names a gap
specific to what you actually did, not a gap every performance in the course
shares.

## 3. Apply the hard case to your own evidence — 6 minutes

The lecture left Farhan's case with two live verdicts: count the pass, on the
grounds the administration was sound and some preparation is normal; or mark
it inconclusive, on the grounds the specific Challenge he answered may have
been rehearsed.

Ask the same question of your own evidence from step 1. Is there a
plausible, ordinary way it could have been produced that would make it much
weaker evidence than it looks — prior practice on the exact question,
memorised phrasing, help from a source you didn't fully record? Write one or
two sentences naming that possibility honestly, even if you believe it did
not happen.

If you genuinely cannot think of a plausible alternative explanation for
your evidence, say why — for example, because the exact question was
provably impossible to anticipate. That is also a legitimate answer, and a
stronger one than most.

## 4. Optional: swap evidence with a partner

If you have a partner, exchange your step-1 evidence and step-2 claims. Each
person tries to find a plausible alternative explanation for the other's
evidence that they did not think of themselves — the way Week 12's examiner
learned about Farhan's rehearsal only from someone outside the exam room.

This adds a second, independent source of scrutiny your own step 3 cannot
supply on its own. It does not remove the core limit: your partner still
cannot observe what actually happened before your evidence was produced,
only propose plausible alternatives to it, exactly as you did for yourself.

## 5. Name what would change your verdict — 4 minutes

Write one sentence: what single additional piece of evidence — a topic you
provably could not have prepared for, a witness to your preparation, a
transcript of what you actually did beforehand — would move your claim from
step 2 toward more certainty, one way or the other?

If you cannot name one, that is itself worth recording: it means your
current verdict may be as settled as this kind of evidence can make it, and
further hedging would not be honesty, just caution for its own sake.

## 6. Keep a short record

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence chosen | What you selected in step 1, and from which week |
| Claim | Your step-2 sentence: what the evidence justifies |
| Limit | Your step-2 sentence: what it does not justify |
| Alternative explanation | Your step-3 answer: a plausible, ordinary way the evidence could be weaker than it looks |
| What would change it | Your step-5 answer |
| Mode | Solo or with a partner, and what that choice added or lost |

This record is the last one this course asks you to keep. Its shape is no
different from Week 1's observation, interpretation and limit — twelve weeks
later, the tool is the same one, applied more carefully.

## 7. Read further

For the general framework behind step 2's two-sentence shape — a claim
supported by a stated warrant, qualified by what it does not establish —
read Part 3 of Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup and FitzGerald's *The Craft of
Research* (University of Chicago Press), particularly its sections on
warrants and on acknowledging and responding to objections. Ask: what is the
difference between qualifying a claim honestly and simply hedging it into
meaninglessness?
