## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided investigation applies [Week 2’s lesson](/lectures/week-02/).
Allow about 30 minutes, plus 15 more if you complete step 7.
Everything you need is on this page. Work alone or with a
partner; no paid book, AI account, special equipment or physical zip is
required, and you should not look anything up until step 4 tells you to. Nadia
and Theo are fictional.

You are examining evidence of understanding, not sitting a test about zips. If
you already know how a zip works, say so in your record rather than pretending
the material is new. If you did the slide exercises, reuse those answers and
start at step 4.

Keep this as practice in judging a performance from evidence produced under stated conditions, qualified by what that evidence cannot establish.

## 1. Rate yourself — 1 minute

On a scale of 1 to 7, how well do you understand how a zip works? 1 is the
lowest level of understanding and 7 the highest. Rozenblit and Keil, whose
protocol this adapts, trained their participants on this scale with a cross bow,
which most adults understand mechanistically, and a satellite navigation
receiver, which most do not.

Record the number as **Rating 1**. Do not revise it later; the point of the
trail is that earlier entries stay as they were.

## 2. Explain it — 6 minutes

Write a detailed, step-by-step causal explanation of how a zip opens and closes.
Name the parts and say what each one does to the others. Someone who had never
seen a zip should be able to follow you. Do not consult anything.

Preserve your original wording. When you finish, re-rate yourself on the same
scale as **Rating 2**.

## 3. Answer the diagnostic — 4 minutes

A **diagnostic question** is one that cannot be answered without the mechanism
itself. Here is ours:

> The slider on a zip is a single rigid piece with no moving parts inside it.
> Pulled one way it closes the zip; pulled the other way it opens it. Explain,
> step by step, how one rigid object can do both.

Write your answer, then record **Rating 3**. Attempt this before reading step 4,
which contains the description.

## 4. Read the description — 3 minutes

Technical background from [Wikipedia’s article on the zipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipper).
It grounds the example; it is not evidence for a claim about understanding.

A zip has two **tapes**, the fabric strips sewn to the garment, each carrying a
row of **teeth** (also called elements) along its inner edge. A **slider** rides
on both rows, moved by a **pull tab**, with a **top stop** and **bottom stop**
at the ends.

Inside the slider is a **Y-shaped channel**. Closing, the two arms of the Y are
inlets and the stem is the outlet, so the rows converge and are pressed together
one pair at a time. Each tooth has a **projection** on one face and a **recess**
on the other; the projection is slightly larger than the recess it enters, so
the tooth springs apart and closes over its neighbour. That spring is an
**elastic deformation** — the tooth returns to its shape, so the joint holds
once the slider has passed, resisting force both across the zip and along it.
Opening, the Y is reversed: the stem is the inlet, the arms the outlets, and the
wedge between them prises each pair apart in turn.

One **boundary condition** follows from that geometry rather than from any extra
fact: the hold depends on every tooth keeping its shape and having an intact
neighbour to grip, so a bent or missing tooth leaves a point where nothing is
interlocked and the chain parts there even though the slider has passed.

<details>
<summary>Compare after attempting the diagnostic</summary>

The slider needs no moving parts because the work is done by a fixed shape and
the direction of travel through it: the same Y is a funnel from one end and a
splitter from the other. Once it has passed, the teeth are held by their own
interlocking geometry, not by the slider.

That is also why a sideways tug on the tapes does not open the zip. It loads
every interlocked pair at once, in the direction the projection-in-recess
geometry resists, whereas the slider reaches the teeth one pair at a time, so
the whole force falls on one joint.

</details>

## 5. Annotate what you wrote — 4 minutes

Set your own explanation beside that description. **Do not rewrite it.** Mark
each relation in the description that your text did not contain:

| Label | What it marks |
| --- | --- |
| Missing relation | A part whose effect on another part you never stated. |
| Missing cause | An outcome named without the mechanism that produces it. |
| Boundary condition | When the mechanism stops working. |
| Dependency | Something a step quietly relies on. |

<details>
<summary>Compare with a fictional annotated example</summary>

Theo wrote: “The slider pulls the two sides of teeth together and they interlock
like fingers. Pulling it back down separates them again.” Every word is true and
it would satisfy most listeners.

*Missing relation:* nothing about what holds one tooth to the next. *Missing
cause:* “interlock” names the outcome, not the projection entering the recess.
*Missing dependency:* no Y-shaped channel, so nothing in the explanation makes
the two directions of travel different from each other.

If your own text already contained most of the description, annotate it that way
and say so. A strong performance is a result, not a problem with the exercise.

</details>

## 6. Examine the evidence — 5 minutes

Keep this record. Later weeks will ask you to compare against it.

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Prior familiarity | What did you already know about zips, and how? |
| Conditions | Written or spoken, alone or with a partner, sources open or closed, time taken. |
| Rating trail | Ratings 1, 2 and 3, unrevised. |
| Original text | Preserved before annotation. |
| Observation | What your explanation actually contained. |
| Interpretation | The specific thing it shows you can produce. |
| Alternative | Could recall of a diagram, a recent repair, or learning during this page explain it? |
| Limit | The specific claim that would still need more evidence. |

Two cautions the lesson turns on. First, the rating trail is **self-report**: a
drop tells you your confidence changed, not what you can produce. The
independent artefact is the annotated text. Second, running this once on
yourself, with no control and no comparison group, is **not a replication** of
Rozenblit and Keil’s study; it is a record of one session.

“I don’t really understand zips” is not a limit. “My text never said what holds
two teeth together” is, because it names the missing evidence.

## 7. Swap, or run the solo route — 15 minutes

**With a partner.** One of you names a topic you genuinely know. The other
researches it freely for ten minutes, then explains it back with sources closed.
The knower probes: the exception, the case where the usual account breaks, the
step everyone skips. The probe is not marking; it is finding where the
explanation stops being load-bearing. Record what was asked and what the
explanation could not reach.

**Alone.** You can do this later. Repeat steps 1 to 6 on a second everyday mechanism — a door handle, a
bicycle brake, a tap — using one freely available technical description at step
4. Then write the single sentence that states the loss: with no second person
there is no independent check on whether your explanation actually satisfied
someone who knows the material. You grade your text against a description you
have just read, so you cannot tell whether you would have produced the missing
step unprompted, and every probe you face is one you chose.

## 8. Finish with the examiner’s sentence

Write one claim your annotated text supports, and one it does not. For example:
“With no sources, I produced a written explanation that named the slider and the
teeth but not the mechanism holding them together. This supports the claim that
I can describe the visible action of a zip. It does not support the claim that I
can explain why the teeth stay joined, and it says nothing about how I would
handle a mechanism I had never used.”

That is the form of judgement this course is practising: an observation, a claim
no wider than the observation, and a stated limit. Next week removes the source
instead of supplying it, and asks what reconstruction reveals that explanation
did not.
