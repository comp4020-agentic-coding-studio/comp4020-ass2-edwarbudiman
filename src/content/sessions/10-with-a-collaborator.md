## Where Week 9 left us

Week 9 preserved a prior claim beside a revised answer, but that pair cannot settle how much assistance contributed. This week adds a live collaborator. A rung on the ladder now describes the observed joint performance; attributing that rung to the person alone requires further evidence.

## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided investigation applies [Week 10's lesson](/lectures/week-10/).
Allow about 30 minutes. Work alone, with a human partner standing in as your
collaborator, or with an AI assistant if you have access to one — instructions
for all three routes are below. Nothing paid is required. Nadia and Theo, from
the lecture's worked example, are fictional; you are not reproducing their
exact exchange, only its structure.

You are examining what a live collaborator does to the evidence a performance
can supply, not testing whether using AI is permitted. There is no version of
this task in which consulting a collaborator counts against you; the point is
to find out, honestly, what the record you produce can and cannot show.

## 1. Choose a topic and write your questions — 5 minutes

Pick a topic you know moderately well but not perfectly — something you could
explain for a few minutes without notes, but where you suspect a harder
question would expose a gap. Avoid this course's own running examples
(Wi-Fi, the zip, the lock, the cyclist, compound interest, baking, honeybee
cooling) so that your answers are genuinely your own and not remembered from
these pages.

Write **three diagnostic questions** about your topic — the kind Nadia asks in
Week 2 and Week 10: not "what is X," but a question that only someone who
actually grasps the mechanism could answer well. Set them aside somewhere you
will not see them again until step 3. Do not consult anything yet.

## 2. Explain the topic, with a collaborator present — 8 minutes

Choose one route.

**With an AI assistant.** Open a chat window and keep it open for this whole
step. Explain your topic aloud or in writing, for up to eight minutes,
consulting the assistant whenever you want — before you speak, mid-sentence,
or not at all. Nothing is disqualified; the point is that it stays available
throughout, not only beforehand.

**With a human partner.** Brief your partner on your topic in advance, from a
source you supply, so they can act as a stand-in collaborator. During your
explanation, they may answer any question you redirect to them, in their own
words, live, exactly as an AI assistant would respond to a typed question.

**Solo, with an AI assistant and no examiner.** Run the same eight minutes
alone, with the assistant open throughout, and no one else present to probe
you. Keep step 4's honesty check in mind: you will be the only judge of your
own transcript.

Keep a rough transcript or notes of what was said as you go — you will need it
in step 4.

## 3. Put your three questions to yourself — 6 minutes

Now retrieve your three diagnostic questions from step 1. Answer each one, in
the same conditions as step 2 (collaborator still available). For each
question, before moving to the next, write one line noting whether you
answered it yourself, relayed the collaborator's answer, or produced some mix.

If you are working with a human partner as your examiner, have them ask the
three questions instead, so the redirect-or-answer choice happens live under
some observation, closer to how Nadia's probing worked in Week 2 and Week 10.

## 4. Build the three-way attribution record — 6 minutes

For each of the three questions, complete this record:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Question | The diagnostic question, as written in step 1. |
| What was said | A short summary or direct quote of your answer. |
| Attribution | Answered alone / relayed from collaborator / mixed — could not fully separate. |
| Confidence in the attribution | How sure are you this label is honest, on a 1–3 scale, and why. |

Be specific rather than generous with yourself. "Mixed" is not a weaker answer
than "alone" — it is frequently the more honest one, and Week 10's lesson is
that honesty here is harder than it looks, not that mixed answers are worse
evidence.

## 5. Name the sentence you can't attribute — 3 minutes

Reread your transcript from step 2 or step 3. Find **one specific line** — not
a general impression, an actual sentence — that you genuinely cannot attribute
to yourself or to the collaborator alone, and explain in two sentences why not.
This is the exercise's real output. A transcript with no such line at all is
worth treating with suspicion: reread it once more before concluding the
attribution problem simply didn't arise for you.

<details>
<summary>Worked example, if you want to compare before writing your own</summary>

Theo's transcript (Week 10's lecture) has a candidate: "that matches what I
read earlier about fanning having a physical limit." It sounds like Theo's own
connection, made independently of the collaborator. But he had just read the
collaborator's humidity answer seconds earlier, and connecting two things you
were just shown is easier than connecting two things you recalled unprompted.
The honest attribution is: probably Theo's synthesis, prompted by a
collaborator's sentence still fresh in view — not cleanly "his" and not
cleanly "the collaborator's."

## 6. Examine what this does and doesn't establish

Answer in your own words, one or two sentences each:

- What does your attribution record support a claim about — your judgement,
  your prior knowledge of the topic, or something else?
- What would you need, that this exercise does not give you, to know whether
  you could have answered your hardest question without the collaborator
  present at all?
- If you ran the solo route: what does having no examiner change about how
  much you can trust your own "answered alone" labels?

## 7. Finish with the examiner's sentence

Write one claim your record supports, and one it does not — the same form
Week 2 asked for. For example: *"With an AI collaborator open throughout, I
answered two of my three diagnostic questions using language I recognised
from my own earlier reading, and relayed one answer close to verbatim. This
supports the claim that I could judge which of the collaborator's answers fit
what I already knew. It does not support the claim that I could have produced
the relayed answer myself, and it says nothing about how I would perform on a
question I had not pre-written and therefore could not anticipate."*

That last qualification is deliberate: every question here was one you wrote
yourself, so none of them was a surprise in the sense Week 8 examined. Week 11
removes that shelter — you will design and administer a real examination to
another person, who will not have written the questions in advance.
