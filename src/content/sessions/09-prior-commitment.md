## Where Week 8 left us

Week 8’s surprise question exposed a limit: the final answer cannot show what existed before the probe. This week adds a prior state to the ladder record. **Pre-commitment** means recording a prediction and reason before checking, then preserving them beside the revision. Our new fictional case changes the setting, while continuing the same question about evidence.

## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided activity applies [Week 9's lesson](/lectures/week-09/).
Allow 20 minutes. All required information is below. Work alone or with a
partner; no real plant, live experiment, paid tool or AI account is
required. The case is fictional. You are examining what a claim made
*before* an answer is known can tell us, not completing a gardening
certification.

The one rule that makes this activity work: **write your prior claim before
reading the reference in step 2, and do not edit it afterward.** If you
read ahead first, the exercise cannot show you what it is designed to show.

## 1. Commit to a prediction — 4 minutes

Marlow has a third plant: a fern kept on a shaded shelf, whose fronds have
turned pale and papery at the tips over the past two weeks. Nothing else
about the fern has changed — same pot, same spot, same watering routine
Marlow remembers using.

Before reading anything else, write:

1. What you think is wrong, in one or two sentences.
2. Your confidence: low, medium, or high.
3. What specific detail in the description led you to that guess.

Preserve this note exactly. You will not get to rewrite it.

## 2. Read the reference — 3 minutes

Pale, papery, browning frond tips on ferns are commonly caused by one of
three things: low humidity (ferns generally need more moisture in the air
than most houseplants), a build-up of minerals from tap water in the soil,
or too much direct light for a shade-loving plant.

A fern with mineral build-up usually also shows a faint white or grey crust
on the soil surface. A fern getting too much light usually shows the damage
concentrated on the side facing the light source, not evenly across the
plant. Low humidity tends to show up evenly across older fronds first.

## 3. Check the detail and revise — 5 minutes

The tip damage on Marlow's fern is even across the plant, worse on the older,
outer fronds, and there is no crust on the soil. The shelf is shaded, so
direct light is an unlikely cause.

Write a revised claim: what you now think is wrong, and which specific
detail from step 2 changed your mind (or confirmed your original guess).

<summary>Compare after writing your revised claim</summary>

The even, older-frond-first pattern with no soil crust points to low
humidity rather than mineral build-up or excess light. If your prior guess
was humidity, you had the right instinct from limited information — say so
plainly. If it was something else, name exactly which detail (the
"no crust," the "older fronds first," or the "shaded shelf ruling out
light") changed your mind. A vague "I just knew" does not count as a
recorded reason.

## 4. Compare without editing — 3 minutes

Place your step 1 note and your step 3 revision side by side. Do not
rewrite step 1, even if it now looks obviously wrong or if you're tempted to
describe it as "basically what I thought." This resistance is the point of
the exercise: Fischhoff's (1975) subjects could not stop their own memory of
a prior judgment from shifting once they knew the outcome, and the only
defence against that is a record made before the shift could happen.

Write one sentence naming what the two notes, together, show that the
revised note alone would not.

## 5. Name the limit — 5 minutes

Keep a short record:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Prior claim | Your step 1 guess and stated confidence. |
| Revised claim | Your step 3 conclusion and the specific detail that changed or confirmed it. |
| What this supports | What the pairing of the two notes demonstrates about your reasoning at each point. |
| What it does not establish | What remains unknown — for example, whether you would reason the same way about an unfamiliar plant, or under real time pressure. |

For example: "I predicted mineral build-up (medium confidence) based on the
tap-water detail; the reference's soil-crust check ruled it out, and the
older-fronds pattern pointed to low humidity instead. This supports that I
can revise a specific claim in light of a specific new detail. It does not
show that I would reach the right answer without a reference to check
against, or that I'd resist convincing myself afterward I'd 'basically known
it,' if I hadn't written the first note down."

## 6. Finish with the examiner's question — 3 minutes

If you were examining someone else's fern diagnosis, and they told you
afterward "I suspected humidity the whole time" with no written note from
before they checked, what would you be unable to conclude from that claim,
and why? Write two sentences.

Working alone, you can verify your own prior note exists and is unedited,
but you cannot independently verify that you didn't glance ahead before
writing it. Working with a partner, one person can watch the other write
step 1 before step 2 is revealed — stronger evidence of a genuine prior
state, though it still cannot rule out the partner having encountered
something similar before this session.

For the study behind this week's caution about self-reported prior belief,
read Fischhoff (1975), ["Hindsight ≠ Foresight," §§ Experiment
1](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Fischhoff_1975_Hindsight_is_not_equal_to_foresight.pdf).
Ask whether a claim you can no longer trust yourself to remember accurately
is still a claim worth asking someone to make.
