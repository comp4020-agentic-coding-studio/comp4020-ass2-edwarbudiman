## Purpose and preparation

This self-guided investigation applies [Week 4's lesson](/lectures/week-04/).
Allow about 25 minutes. Everything you need is on this page — you do not need
the lecture open, a lock, any tools, or any paid or AI resource. Work alone or
with a partner. Rowan and the three responses are fictional teaching examples
written for this lesson, not transcripts and not research data.

You are practising a judgement, not grading a person. If you decide Rowan
understands the lock, that is a result. The purpose is to find out what your
judgement rested on, which is a different question from whether it was right.

If you have already read all three responses in the lecture or the deck, you
cannot un-read them. Note that as a condition of your own judging and carry on;
first impressions that have already formed are exactly the kind of thing this
week is about.

## 1. The lock, in brief — 3 minutes

A **pin tumbler lock** has a fixed outer **casing**, and inside it a cylinder
that can rotate, called the **plug**. The key enters the **keyway** at the front
of the plug. At the back of the plug a **cam** pulls the bolt out of the door
frame when the plug turns.

Across the boundary between plug and casing sits a row of small stacks, usually
five or six. Each stack is a **key pin** at the bottom, resting in the plug; a
**driver pin** above it; and a spring above that, pressing the stack down. The
**shear line** is that boundary itself — the join where the outside of the plug
meets the casing. It is not a part; it is the line the plug must turn along.

With no key in, the springs push each stack down so the driver pins cross the
shear line into the plug and hold it. A correctly cut key lifts each key pin by
exactly the amount that puts the junction between that key pin and its driver
pin level with the shear line. Nothing then crosses the boundary, so the plug
turns and the bolt withdraws. A wrong key lifts the stacks by the wrong amounts:
too little and a driver pin still hangs into the plug, too far and a key pin is
pushed up into the casing. Either way something crosses the shear line. There is
no partly open — one crossing anywhere is enough.

Technical grounding: [Wikipedia, "Pin tumbler lock"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock).
Keep this section visible for the whole activity; nothing here is a memory test.

## 2. What Rowan was asked, and what Rowan said — 5 minutes

The same fictional person, the same lock, the same afternoon, three conditions.
A **condition** is a feature of the situation an answer was produced in that
could itself change the answer: what could be consulted, who the answer was for,
and what kind of question was asked.

**Condition (a) — the reference visible.** Rowan has the text in section 1 open
on the table, and is asked to explain how a pin tumbler lock works.

> A pin tumbler lock has a plug that rotates inside a fixed casing. The key goes
> into the keyway at the front of the plug. Along the boundary between the two
> there are five or six stacks of pins, and each stack is a key pin at the
> bottom, sitting in the plug, a driver pin above it, and a spring above that
> pressing down.
>
> That boundary is called the shear line. With no key in, the springs push the
> stacks down so the driver pins cross the shear line into the plug, and that is
> what stops it turning.
>
> The key pins are different lengths, and the key is cut to match them. The
> correct key lifts each key pin so that the junction between the key pin and
> the driver pin ends up exactly at the shear line. Then nothing is crossing the
> boundary, the plug turns, and the cam at the back withdraws the bolt. A wrong
> key lifts the pins by the wrong amounts, so something still crosses the shear
> line and it stays locked.

**Condition (b) — from memory, to a novice.** The reference is removed. The
listener has never wondered what happens inside a key.

> OK, so — you know the zigzag notches along the top edge of a key? They are not
> decoration. They are a code, and the lock is the thing that reads it.
>
> Inside the lock there is a barrel that has to spin round for the door to open.
> Normally it cannot, because there is a row of little pins, five or six of
> them, sticking down through the join between the barrel and the metal around
> it. A pin sitting across a join is a bolt. Nothing is turning.
>
> Every pin is a different length and every notch is a different depth. The
> right key lifts each pin by exactly its own amount, so all of them come flush
> with that join at the same moment. Nothing is sticking across it any more, the
> barrel spins, and a little arm at the back pulls the bolt out of the frame.
>
> A wrong key does lift the pins. It just lifts them to the wrong heights, so
> one is always left poking across the join, and it stays shut.

**Condition (c) — a prediction about a changed case.** Still nothing to consult.
Rowan is told that on a key which works this lock, one single cut — the third
one along — has been filed a little too shallow, and every other cut is correct.
What happens when it is turned, and why?

> Sorry — do you want me to guess, or is there a right answer? … Right. OK.
>
> Too shallow means it does not lift that pin far enough. So that one is sitting
> low. The bit of it that is supposed to end up level with the join is below the
> join instead, so something is still crossing, at that one position.
>
> And — this is the bit I am least sure about — I think it does not matter that
> the other five are fine. One is enough. Because the barrel is one piece. It is
> not five separate locks that each open a fifth of the way. So it does not half
> turn or feel loose. It just does not turn, the same as a completely wrong key.
>
> Hold on. Would you even be able to tell this key from a totally wrong one,
> from the outside? I do not think you would. That feels wrong to me but I
> cannot see what would make it false.

## 3. Judge each response — 6 minutes

Fill this in before reading the comparison. Preserve your original wording; if
you change your mind later, write the second version underneath rather than
replacing the first.

| Response | This gives me evidence of… | What it cannot rule out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (a) reference visible | | |
| (b) from memory, to a novice | | |
| (c) prediction about a changed key | | |

Then write one sentence answering the blunt question: **does Rowan understand
how this lock works?**

<details>
<summary>Compare after you have written all four</summary>

**(a)** Evidence that Rowan can assemble a correct, complete, correctly ordered
account with the text available, using every term properly. It cannot rule out
well-organised reading: we have no independent record of how much Rowan
consulted, and the order and vocabulary follow the reference closely.

**(b)** Evidence that something survives the removal of the source and can be
recast for a new audience — including one relation phrased in Rowan's own words
and not in the reference ("a pin sitting across a join is a bolt"). But it also
contains a mechanical error: Rowan describes a single pin per position being
lifted "flush with the join." What has to reach the shear line is the *junction
between two pins*, not a pin. With one pin per position, lifting it too far
would carry it clear of the join and the lock would open, so Rowan's model has
no way to fail by over-lifting — and sure enough, Rowan's wrong key only ever
lifts too little.

**(c)** Evidence that cannot have been memorised, because this key does not
exist. Measured against section 1 it is also correct, and it reaches the
consequence that matters: one wrong stack is enough,
because the plug is a single object. That is a causal relation, not a part name.
But it is hesitant, heavily hedged, and carries the same vagueness as (b) about
which part is supposed to reach the boundary.

**A sharper objection to (c).** Section 1 — the reference Rowan had under
condition (a) — already says that one crossing anywhere is enough. So the
headline conclusion of (c) was available in print, and "it could not have been
memorised" is too quick. What section 1 does *not* supply is Rowan's reason:
that the plug is one object, so there is no partial turn and no way to tell the
difference from outside. That inference is the part of (c) with no source.

**The blunt question.** There is a defensible case on both sides, built from
these same three texts. Looking harder at Rowan will not settle it, because what
divides the two cases is which condition you let count.

</details>

## 4. Choose a condition and state your rule — 4 minutes

Write down which single condition you would trust most, one sentence of
reasoning, and one sentence giving the strongest objection to your own choice.

Then finish this sentence in one line, and keep it: **"I counted that as
stronger evidence because…"**

<details>
<summary>Three defensible choices, each with its cost</summary>

**(a)** is the only condition where Rowan can be checked against the mechanism
without memory interfering, so an error there is unambiguous — but it cannot
separate understanding from careful reading, which is the distinction we wanted.

**(b)** removes the source and changes the audience, so whatever survives is
Rowan's own — but an explanation rehearsed for a novice is exactly the kind that
can be fluent and wrong, and this one was.

**(c)** could not have existed in advance, so a good answer cannot be explained
away — but it is the condition most likely to fail for reasons that have nothing
to do with the lock. Rowan's opening question ("do you want me to guess?")
suggests being asked to predict at all was unfamiliar. A weak answer under (c)
is ambiguous between having no causal model and having no experience of being
asked.

If your sentence came out as "it just seemed more convincing," write that down
rather than improving it. That is the finding, and it is what Week 5 exists to
supply.

</details>

## 5. Design a fourth condition — 4 minutes

Invent one more condition for the same lock and the same person. Change one
thing only: what may be consulted, who the answer is for, how much warning there
is, or what kind of question is asked.

Write three lines: the condition, what evidence you expect it to produce that
the first three did not, and the most likely way it could fail for a reason
unconnected to the lock.

<details>
<summary>One worked example</summary>

**Condition:** Rowan is shown a lock that will not open and asked to name two
different faults that could cause it, then say which observation would tell them
apart.

**Evidence it could produce:** whether Rowan can run the mechanism backwards,
from a symptom to candidate causes, which neither explaining nor a single
forward prediction requires.

**How it could fail for an unrelated reason:** "name two faults" rewards someone
who has seen broken locks before. Rowan could hold the mechanism perfectly and
produce one cause, because generating alternatives is a separate skill from
having the model.

Your fourth condition does not have to resemble this one. What makes it a good
one is that you can say in advance what it would add and how it could mislead
you.

</details>

## 6. Examine your own judging — 3 minutes

Keep this record. It is the thing you carry out of Week 4.

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Order | Which response you read or judged first, and whether your later readings argued with it. |
| The evidence | Which response moved your verdict most. |
| The condition | What that condition made possible, and what it ruled out. |
| The rule | Your one-line sentence from section 4, unedited. |
| Consistency | Did your three judgements pull the same way, and what would have had to be different for one of them to pull the other way? |
| The limit | One thing your verdict does not establish about Rowan. |

On consistency, be careful about what a clean result means. Three judgements
pointing the same way show that you applied something stable. They do not show
that the something was a good criterion, or that you could state it. The common
version is forming a view under one condition and reading the other two through
it without registering that a condition was chosen at all. If nothing would have
changed your verdict, the consistency came from a commitment rather than from
the evidence.

## 7. Working with a partner, and working alone

**With a partner.** Both judge all three responses independently and in writing
before comparing. Then find the first point at which your records diverge and
try to state it as a disagreement about *which condition counts*, rather than
about Rowan. Neither of you has to concede.

**Alone.** Do sections 3 to 6 as written, then leave the page and come back to
it later in the day. Re-judge response (c) on its own, without rereading (a) and
(b), and compare with your first record.

The solo route loses something real and it is worth naming: a second judgement
by you is not an independent judgement. You already know your first verdict, and
a partner's disagreement is evidence you cannot manufacture for yourself. Your
own account of why you judged as you did is self-report, not an observation of
how the judgement was produced.

## What this investigation does and does not establish

You have produced a written judgement, a stated reason and a named objection,
which is more than an intuition and can be inspected by someone else. You have
not established that your rule is a good one, because nothing here tested it
against anyone else's.

That is the gap Week 5 addresses, by building an explicit instrument: a stated
ladder of performances with each rung defined well enough that two people can
apply it and locate their disagreement. Do not draft one yet — keep your
one-line rule from section 4 exactly as you wrote it, and bring it. It is the
raw material.
