> You have used a zip thousands of times. Could you explain how one works?

This is a self-guided lesson. These notes and the [slide deck](/decks/week-02/)
each contain the explanations you need; no presenter is assumed. Allow about 40
minutes, with another 30 if you complete the [Week 2 investigation](/sessions/02-first-review/).
Use paper or a text document, because this lesson asks you to produce a text and
then examine it. You do not need to own a zip, hold one, or look one up: a full
explanation of how a zip works appears in section 4, at the point where the
activity has finished with it. Nadia, Theo and their exchange are fictional.

## What you will learn

By the end you should be able to distinguish recognition and familiarity from
what a person can produce; run an adapted rate, explain and re-rate protocol on
yourself; annotate an explanation for missing relations, causes, boundary
conditions and dependencies; and say why a drop in self-rating is not, on its
own, evidence about performance.

## 1. Where Week 1 left us

Week 1 introduced the **examiner**, the person deciding what a performance
demonstrates. It separated an **observation** from an **inference**, insisted
that the conditions an answer was produced under belong in the record, and used
Clark and Chalmers to argue that consulting a reference does not automatically
disqualify a performance. It ended on a problem: a correct sentence did not tell
us what the speaker could produce independently.

The obvious next move is to stop accepting sentences and ask for explanations.
An explanation has parts — steps, causes, things that depend on other things —
so there is more in it to inspect. This week tests that move and finds its
limit. Explaining is a useful **probe**, a way of generating evidence, but it is
not a **proof**.

Two feelings need separating before we start. **Recognition** is knowing that
you have met something before; you recognise a zip instantly. **Familiarity** is
the broader sense that something is well known to you and easy to follow, which
is what Week 1 met in a Wi-Fi explanation that “made sense” on reading. Neither
feeling reports on what you could produce. The rest of this lesson is about how
you would find that out about yourself.

## 2. The protocol, and where it comes from

Rozenblit and Keil (2002) asked people to rate how well they understood an
everyday object, write a detailed explanation of it, then rate themselves again;
a question about the mechanism followed, and then a third rating. Their eight
test items included a speedometer, a flush toilet, a cylinder lock — and a
zipper, which is why a zip is this week’s continuing example.

What follows is **our adaptation** of their protocol, run by one reader on one
object. Keep that label in mind; section 6 explains why it matters.

## 3. Run it on yourself

### Rating 1 — one minute

On a scale of 1 to 7, how well do you understand how a zip works? 1 is the
lowest level of understanding and 7 the highest. Rozenblit and Keil trained
their participants on this scale using a cross bow, which most adults understand
mechanistically, and a satellite navigation receiver, which most do not. Write
your number down as **Rating 1** and do not change it later.

### The explanation — six minutes

Write a detailed, step-by-step causal explanation of how a zip opens and closes.
Name the parts and say what each one does to the others; someone who had never
seen a zip should be able to follow you. Do not look anything up. When you
finish, rate yourself again on the same scale as **Rating 2**.

Whatever you wrote, it is now a text with edges. Writing forces an explanation
out of the state in which it felt complete and into something with a beginning,
an end, and gaps you can point at. In Rozenblit and Keil’s studies, self-ratings
dropped after this step. Compare your two numbers now; a fall, a rise and no
change are all informative.

### The diagnostic question — four minutes

Rozenblit and Keil followed the explanation with a **diagnostic question**: one
that cannot be answered without the mechanism. For the cylinder lock they asked
participants to explain, step by step, how one could pick it. Ours:

> The slider on a zip is a single rigid piece with no moving parts inside it.
> Pulled one way it closes the zip; pulled the other way it opens it. Explain,
> step by step, how one rigid object can do both.

Write your answer, then re-rate yourself as **Rating 3**. Attempt it before
reading section 4, which contains the answer.

## 4. How a zip actually works

At this point in their protocol, Rozenblit and Keil gave participants a brief
expert description and asked them to re-rate. Here is ours. The technical
background throughout this section is
[Wikipedia’s article on the zipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipper). An
encyclopaedia article is adequate background for a mechanism of this kind; it
grounds the example and is not evidence for any claim about understanding.

**The parts.** A zip has two **tapes**, the strips of fabric sewn to the garment
or bag. Along the inner edge of each tape runs a row of **teeth**, also called
elements, either as separate pieces or as one continuous coil. The **slider**
rides on both rows and is moved by a **pull tab**. A **top stop** keeps the
slider from running off the end, and a **bottom stop** holds the two tapes
together at the base. Separating zips replace the bottom stop with an insertion
pin and a retainer box.

**Closing.** Inside the slider is a **Y-shaped channel**. Run the slider in the
closing direction and the two arms of the Y are its inlets while the single stem
is its outlet, so the two rows of teeth converge and are pressed together one
pair at a time. Each tooth carries a small **projection** on one face and a
matching **recess** on the other. The projection is slightly larger than the
recess it enters, so the tooth springs apart and closes over its neighbour, in
the manner of a hair pin’s jaw.

**Holding, and opening.** That spring is an **elastic deformation**: the tooth
returns to its own shape afterwards, so the joint is a snug fit that holds once
the slider has moved on. Interlocked projections and recesses resist being
pulled apart both across the zip and along it. Run the slider the other way and
the Y is reversed — the stem becomes the inlet, the arms the outlets, and the
wedge between them prises each pair of teeth apart in turn.

**One boundary condition.** A **boundary condition** is the circumstance in
which a mechanism stops working, and this one follows from the geometry above
rather than from any extra fact. The hold depends on every tooth keeping its own
shape and having an intact neighbour to grip. Bend one tooth, or lose one, and
at that point nothing is interlocked, so the chain parts there even though the
slider has passed.

<details>
<summary>Worked response: how one rigid object can do both</summary>

The slider needs no moving parts because the work is done by a fixed shape and
by the direction of travel through it. The same Y is a funnel from one end and a
splitter from the other, so teeth are brought together or forced apart depending
on which way they enter it.

The teeth then do the rest. They are not held by the slider once it has passed;
they are held by their own interlocking geometry. That is also why a sideways
tug on the two tapes does not open the zip: it loads every interlocked pair at
once, in the direction the projection-in-recess geometry is shaped to resist,
whereas the slider reaches the teeth one pair at a time, so the whole of the
force falls on a single joint. The same fastening, undone in series rather than
resisted in parallel.

## 5. Annotate your own explanation

Allow four minutes. Set your explanation beside the description above and do not
rewrite it. Mark every relation in the description that your text did not
contain, using four labels:

| Label | What it marks |
| --- | --- |
| Missing relation | A part whose effect on another part you never stated. |
| Missing cause | An outcome named without the mechanism that produces it. |
| Boundary condition | When the mechanism stops working. |
| Dependency | Something a step quietly relies on. |

Your output is an annotated copy of your original text. Keep the original
wording; the annotations go in the margin.

<details>
<summary>Worked response: a fictional explanation, annotated</summary>

Theo wrote: “The slider pulls the two sides of teeth together and they interlock
like fingers. Pulling it back down separates them again.”

Every word of that is true, and it would satisfy most listeners. Annotated, it
is thin. *Missing relation:* nothing about what holds one tooth to the next.
*Missing cause:* “interlock” names the outcome rather than the projection
entering the recess. *Missing dependency:* no Y-shaped channel, so nothing in
the explanation makes the two directions of travel different from each other.

</details>

Theo’s sentence is confident, grammatical and correct. It is also the shape of
explanation that hides a gap: it names an outcome in place of the mechanism that
produces it. **This is the limitation Week 2 exists to expose. A fluent
explanation can conceal missing relations, missing causes, boundary conditions
and dependencies.** Fluency is a property of the telling, not of the model
behind it.

Notice what that does not say. It does not say explanations are worthless, that
everybody fails, or that a confident speaker is probably bluffing. It says the
telling and the model can come apart, so the telling on its own cannot settle
the question.

### If your explanation held up

Some readers will have written most of section 4 already. Take that seriously
rather than explaining it away: you produced, unaided and on demand, a causal
account that survived comparison with a technical one. Then say precisely what
it establishes — that you could produce this explanation, of this object, in
writing, at this moment, with nothing in front of you. It does not establish
that you would survive a probe you had not anticipated, or that the same holds
for any other mechanism. A strong performance still has to be described
accurately; that is the same discipline as describing a weak one.

## 6. What the study shows, and what our version does not

Rozenblit and Keil call this the **illusion of explanatory depth**: people
believe they understand how everyday things work in more mechanistic detail than
they can actually produce, and that belief shrinks as soon as they try to
explain. In Study 1, sixteen graduate students at one university initially rated 48 items on the
1 to 7 scale. Each then explained four test items from one of two sets; those
items had five ratings in all. We have used the first three rating stages. A repeated-measures ANOVA found the effect of time significant,
F(4, 56) = 16.195, p < .001. The rating taken after explaining, the rating after
the diagnostic question, and the re-rating of prior knowledge after reading an
expert description were each lower than the first, p < .002. A fifth rating, of
current knowledge after reading that description, rose again, so participants
were not simply losing confidence in everything.

This is an **empirical finding**, demonstrated across twelve studies in the one
paper. It is not a theory of understanding and not a settled consensus. Study 1
alone is sixteen graduate students at one institution; the paper’s broader
claims rest on the other studies too.

### Our critique: a rating is not a performance

This point is ours, not Rozenblit and Keil’s. The thing that fell in their study
was a **self-rating**. If your Rating 3 fell below Rating 1, you have evidence
that your confidence changed. You do not yet have evidence about your
performance. Your session produced two quite different artefacts:

| Artefact | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Your three ratings | A record of how confident you felt at three moments. Self-report; it moves with mood, with how hard the question sounded, and with your guess about what this lesson wants. |
| Your written explanation | A text that exists whatever you now feel about it, readable by someone who is not you and comparable line by line against a technical description. |

Only the second is independent of your own account of yourself. Week 1 already
warned that a feeling of familiarity is not an observation of what someone can
do; the same warning applies to a feeling of ignorance.

### Our version is not a replication

Running this on yourself, once, with no control condition and nobody to compare
against, is not a replication of Rozenblit and Keil, and should never be
described as one. You also knew the design before you started, which is exactly
what their participants did not. Treat your three ratings as the record of one
person’s session, useful for examining yourself. Their studies are the evidence
for the general claim; your session is not.

## 7. The knowledge paper-swap

The in-class version puts two people in the roles the protocol above made you
play at once. **Nadia** names a topic she genuinely knows: the thing she does at
work, an instrument she plays, a process she has run a hundred times. **Theo**
researches it freely for ten minutes, using whatever he likes, and then explains
it back to Nadia with his sources closed. Nadia probes.

Nadia has something no self-check has. She knows what a complete answer contains
and she did not learn it from the page Theo read five minutes ago. Her probes
come from the real structure of the topic: the exception, the case where the
usual account breaks, the step everyone skips. If Theo’s explanation survives
those, somebody who knows the material was satisfied by it — an observation, and
a stronger one than Theo’s own sense of how it went. She is not marking him and
not trying to catch him out; she is finding where his explanation stops being
load-bearing.

### The solo route, and what it loses

Working alone, choose a second everyday mechanism — a door handle, a bicycle
brake, a tap. Rate yourself, write the step-by-step causal explanation, re-rate,
then find one freely available technical description and annotate your text
against it exactly as you did for the zip. Allow fifteen minutes; it can wait
until after the lecture.

State the loss exactly: **with no second person there is no independent check on
whether your explanation actually satisfied someone who knows the material.**
You grade your text against a description you have only just read, so you cannot
tell whether you would have produced the missing step unprompted. And every
probe you meet is one you thought of yourself, so you are never surprised. A
later week returns to what an unforeseen question reveals.

## 8. Keep a short evidence record

Four lines, kept, because later weeks will ask you to compare against them:

1. **Ratings:** 1, 2 and 3, with the conditions — written, alone, no sources.
2. **Observation:** what your explanation actually contained.
3. **Interpretation:** the specific thing it shows you can produce.
4. **Limit:** the specific thing it does not show.

“I don’t really understand zips” is not a limit; it is a mood. “My text never
said what holds two teeth together” is a limit, because it names the missing
evidence and tells you what to collect next.

## What this leaves unresolved

Explaining exposed gaps that a repeated sentence would have hidden, but the
conditions were generous: unlimited time, no interruption, and a lifetime of
handling zips to draw on. So Week 3 changes the conditions. Study something,
lose access to it, then rebuild it. Its question is what becomes visible when
the answer is no longer in front of us, and the claim to test is that
reconstruction is harder to fake than explanation.
