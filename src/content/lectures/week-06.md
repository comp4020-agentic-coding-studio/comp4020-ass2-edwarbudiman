## Where Week 5 left us

Week 5 built a seven-rung ladder, but Recall and Reconstruction assume that removing a source makes the evidence stronger. This week tests that assumption by varying permitted resources. We move from the lock to a fictional courier; the examination question continues even though the setting changes.

> Does needing support show that you do not understand?

This is a self-guided lesson. Both these notes and the slide deck contain the
explanations you need; no presenter is assumed. Allow about 30 minutes, with
another 15–20 minutes if you complete the investigation. Use paper or a text
document for your answers. No navigation app, bicycle or AI account is
required. The people and incidents below are fictional.

## What you will learn

By the end, you should be able to explain why removing a resource is not
automatically a fairer test, name a concrete set of conditions under which an
external resource functions as part of a person's own reasoning rather than
merely assisting it, and design a task that tests engagement with a resource
instead of memory of one specific trip.

## 1. A courier and a phone

Elena works as a bicycle courier. Her phone gives turn-by-turn directions to
each delivery address. Today's delivery is three suburbs from her usual route,
somewhere she has never ridden before. She follows the directions, arrives on
time, and hands over the parcel.

Did Elena demonstrate that she understands the area she just rode through? The
question is not whether she followed the directions correctly — she did — but
what following them correctly is evidence *of*. An examiner watching only the
successful delivery cannot yet tell whether Elena engaged with the route at
all, or simply executed instructions the way a machine would.

## 2. The intuitive move, and why it is not enough

The obvious next step is to take the phone away and ask Elena to do it again.
If she can, the reasoning goes, she really knew the route; if she cannot, she
was only following the tool. Week 5's ladder makes judgements like this
systematic by naming stages a performance can reach — but it carries a hidden
assumption worth testing directly: that performing *without* a resource is
automatically stronger evidence than performing *with* one.

That assumption has a cost. Suppose Elena is handed a blank map immediately
after the delivery and asked to draw the route she just rode. She might manage
a rough sketch — not because she understands the area, but because a single
recent trip sits briefly in short-term memory regardless of whether she
understood anything about it. Conversely, she might fail to reproduce it, not
because she lacks navigational competence, but because nobody remembers a
one-off route from a single ride. Either outcome would be read as evidence
about her navigation, when it is really evidence about recall of one episode.
**Removing support can reveal dependence, but it can just as easily test
memory instead of the ability we actually meant to examine.**

## 3. A resource that thinks with you, not just for you

Clark and Chalmers' *The Extended Mind* argues that an external resource is
not automatically outside the boundary of someone's own thinking. Their
example is Otto, a man with memory loss who carries a notebook and looks up
addresses in it the way another person would recall them from memory. They
ask: if Otto's notebook plays exactly the functional role that biological
memory plays for someone else, on what principled basis do we say Otto's
belief is not really his?

Their answer is not "any resource counts." In §4 they give four conditions
under which an external resource is doing the same job as a memory rather than
merely being consulted like a book: the resource is a **constant** presence
the person turns to whenever it is relevant, rather than an occasional aid;
the information is **available without difficulty**, not buried behind search
or negotiation; retrieved information is **automatically endorsed**, taken as
reliable without a separate act of checking each time; and it was placed there
by the person's own **past endorsement** — it is there because they put it
there, not because someone else's judgement sits inside it. They treat the
fourth condition as more arguable than the first three, but hold that all four
matter.

<p className="source-note">Clark &amp; Chalmers (1998), §4, "From Cognition to
Mind." A theoretical argument, not an empirical finding — it changes what
question we should ask about a resource, not what any single case proves.</p>

## 4. Applying the test to Elena's phone

Run Elena's phone against the same four conditions. Is it a **constant**
presence — does she check it for most unfamiliar routes, or only sometimes?
Is it **available without difficulty** — instant, or does she have to stop,
unlock the phone and search? Does she **automatically endorse** what it says,
the way you would trust your own sense of a familiar street, or does she treat
every instruction as a claim to be checked? And did she **place the
information there herself** — did she choose this app and these settings — or
is she being handed someone else's judgement with no history of her own
endorsement behind it?

For a courier who works the app all shift, every day, checking it instantly
and trusting it without a second thought because she chose and configured it
herself, the first three conditions are clearly met. That does not settle
whether Elena "really" understands the city. It reframes the question: instead
of asking whether the phone disqualifies her performance, we ask whether her
use of the phone is the kind of use that counts as part of her own competence,
or the kind that does not.

## 5. Pause and write: sort three cases

Allow four minutes. For each case below, decide whether the resource looks
more like Otto's notebook (integrated, counts toward the person's own
competence) or more like a stranger's advice consulted once (external,
doesn't). Give one sentence of reasoning for each, using the four conditions.

1. A tourist checks a paper map for the first time, at an intersection, having
   never seen it before this trip.
2. A pharmacist looks up an unfamiliar drug interaction in a reference she
   consults dozens of times a shift, trusts without re-verifying, and chose to
   keep on her desk.
3. A student pastes an essay question into an AI tool they opened for the
   first time five minutes ago and copies out whatever it returns.

<details>
<summary>Compare your answer with a worked response</summary>

**1. The tourist and the paper map** fails constancy and past endorsement: this
is a first use, not a habitual one, and the map was handed to them rather than
assembled from their own prior choices. This looks like consulting a stranger,
not extending one's own memory.

**2. The pharmacist and her reference** meets all three of the strong
conditions: constant, instantly available, automatically trusted because
years of use have earned that trust, and it is a resource she selected and
maintains. This is closer to Otto's notebook — though it says nothing about
whether she could diagnose the interaction unaided, which is a separate
question a different task would have to ask.

**3. The student and the AI tool** fails constancy and past endorsement in the
same way as the tourist, and adds a further problem: the output was not
checked before being copied out, so even automatic endorsement is doing the
wrong job here — trusting an unfamiliar source without verification, not
trusting a well-tested one.

</details>

## 6. What the sorting exercise does and does not show

Sorting these three cases does not tell us that the pharmacist understands
pharmacology, that the tourist cannot read a map, or that the student
understands nothing about the essay topic. It tells us which of the three
resources is doing a job similar to memory, and which is closer to consulting
an outside party for the first time. That distinction changes what a
*supported* performance is evidence of — it does not replace the need to
examine the performance itself.

This is why Week 5's assumption needed testing rather than simply accepting
it: "no resource" and "resource present" are not two clean categories with
understanding on one side. A habitual, self-chosen, trusted resource can
function as part of someone's own reasoning; a fresh, unfamiliar one, even if
technically permitted, mostly is not.

## 7. A better test than removing the phone

If taking Elena's phone away risks testing recall of one trip instead of
navigational competence, what would test the thing we actually want to know?

Change the *content* of the support rather than its presence. Suppose the
phone instructs Elena to turn right onto a street that a visible sign marks as
one-way, entering against traffic. Does she follow the instruction anyway, or
does she notice the conflict and choose a different way to the same address?

This does not remove the resource. It tests whether Elena is engaging with the
route as she rides — reading the street the phone is describing — or treating
the instructions as commands to execute without reference to anything in
front of her. A courier who stops and reroutes has shown something about her
own judgement that a successful, uneventful delivery could not show, whether
or not she ever consults the phone again after this shift.

## 8. Pause and write: design the better test

Allow five minutes. Pick a task you know something about that ordinarily
involves a resource — a recipe app used while cooking a familiar dish, a
calculator used during a familiar kind of problem, a reference chord chart
used while playing a familiar song. Design one change to the *content* of the
resource, not its presence or absence, that would let you tell whether someone
is engaging with the task or just executing instructions. State what a
response to your change would demonstrate, and what it still would not.

<details>
<summary>Worked response: a comparable example</summary>

A calculator used for a familiar kind of problem, e.g. finding a percentage
discount, is a constant, instantly available, automatically trusted, self-chosen
tool for most adults — it passes the four conditions and its presence alone
tells us little. A content change that tests engagement: ask for the discount
on a price already including tax, when the discount only applies pre-tax.
Blind entry of the sticker price produces a plausible-looking wrong answer; a
person tracking what the numbers represent notices the mismatch before
reaching for the keys. This demonstrates whether the person is reasoning about
what the calculation represents, not merely whether they can operate a
calculator. It does not demonstrate that they could solve an unfamiliar kind
of percentage problem with no worked structure at all — that would need a
different, harder change.

</details>

## 9. Keep the record honest

As in Week 1, record the condition rather than treating a supported success as
disqualified or an unsupported failure as proof of ignorance:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Resource | What was available, and how habitual is it for this person? |
| Fit against the four conditions | Constant? Available without difficulty? Automatically endorsed? Self-placed? |
| What was actually tested | Presence/absence of the resource, or engagement with its content? |
| Limit | What would still require a different task to examine? |

A record that only says "used a phone" or "used no phone" is not useful on its
own. A record that says which of the four conditions the resource met, and
whether the task tested content or presence, is inspectable in the way this
course keeps asking for.

## 10. Apply the lesson yourself

Use [the Week 6 investigation](/sessions/06-what-you-can-use/) to design and
record your own content-change test, and to keep a comparison between a
presence/absence version and a content version of the same task.

Before you finish, explain these distinctions in your own words:

- Why an unsupported performance is not automatically stronger evidence than a
  supported one.
- The difference between a resource that is a constant, trusted, self-chosen
  part of someone's routine and one consulted for the first time under
  examination.
- The difference between testing whether a resource is present and testing
  whether someone is engaging with what it says.

## Reading with a purpose

**Primary argument:** Clark, A., and Chalmers, D. (1998). "The Extended Mind."
*Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.
[Read the authors' text](https://www.consc.net/papers/extended.html), §4,
"From Cognition to Mind," and §5, "Beyond the Outer Limits." Ask: which of the
four conditions is doing the most work in distinguishing Otto's notebook from
a resource you would not want to count? We use this paper because it gives an
explicit, arguable test for when a resource is part of someone's own thinking,
rather than leaving "using a resource" as a single undifferentiated category.

## What this leaves unresolved

Sorting resources into "integrated" and "not yet integrated" tells us how to
read a supported performance; it does not, by itself, tell us whether the
underlying reasoning is any good. Next week keeps the resource question open
and asks a different one: if someone can present the same idea in an
unfamiliar form — a new audience, a new representation, a task they were not
drilled on — is that better evidence than either a supported or an unsupported
repetition of the same rehearsed version.
