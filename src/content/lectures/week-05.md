## Two choices in building this ladder

Week 2 used recognition to mean a feeling of familiarity. Rung 1 now requires an observable recognition response; that is limited evidence, not the feeling alone. Application and connection were candidate performances in our plan. Here we judge them through the defined rungs—for example, explaining a new case or justifying a connection—rather than making them separate rungs.

This is a self-guided lesson. These notes and the [slide deck](/decks/week-05/)
each contain the explanations you need; no presenter is assumed. Allow about 40
minutes, with another 25 if you complete the
[Week 5 investigation](/sessions/05-the-ladder/). Use paper or a text document.
You need nothing from Weeks 1 to 4 open in front of you — every performance this
lesson uses is quoted again below — but it helps to have read them once. Sam,
Theo, Priya and Rowan are the fictional people from those weeks; nothing
new is invented about any of them here.

## What you will learn

By the end you should be able to state the seven rungs of the course's ladder
with an operational definition and a real example for each; apply it to a
performance you did not produce yourself and expect to disagree with another
reader about a genuine case; name one rung the last four weeks never produced
evidence for, and say why that gap matters; and state the assumption the ladder
makes by how its own rungs are worded, which next week attacks directly.

## 1. Where Week 4 left us

Week 4 gave one person, Rowan, three performances on one lock under three
conditions, and found that two careful readers could reach opposite verdicts
from the same texts with no way to show each other where they went wrong. It
closed by naming what was missing: not more evidence, but a rule for weighing
the evidence already in hand — "a rule, written down before you look, saying
what counts as stronger and weaker evidence, so that it can be inspected,
applied by someone else, and argued with." Week 4 also previewed this loosely,
as a ladder running "from recognising something up to connecting it to other
things." That phrase was a placeholder; naming the actual top rung is this
week's job, not last week's. Here is the precise version.

## 2. What an instrument is, and is not

An **instrument**, as this course uses the word, is a stated rule for turning a
performance into a judgement, written before you look at the case it will be
applied to. It is not a theory of what understanding *is* — it does not explain
why some performances are harder to produce than others, and it makes no claim
about what happens inside a learner's head. It is a tool for making your own
judgement inspectable: so that when you and another reader disagree, you can
each point at which rung you assigned and argue about that, rather than trading
impressions of how convincing someone sounded.

Week 1's evidence record ("Observation / Interpretation / Limit") was already a
small instrument, and Week 3's four-letter annotation scheme (P / R / C / X) was
a second one, built for one specific kind of performance. This week's ladder is
broader: a single scale you can apply to a recognition, an explanation, a
prediction, almost any performance the course produces from here on.

## 3. The ladder, rung by rung

Each rung below is defined by what a performance has to *do*, not by how
confident, fluent or long it is. A short, hesitant performance can reach a high
rung; a long, fluent one can fail to reach a low one. Section 5 tests that claim
directly.

**1. Recognition** — *"I've seen this."* The person correctly matches or
repeats something already stated, with it available to consult or freshly in
view. This is the weakest rung: it requires identifying, not producing.

**2. Recall** — *"I can retrieve this without the source in front of me."* The
person produces a specific piece of the material — a term, a fact, a step — from
memory, with nothing to consult. Recall returns material; it does not yet
require the material to be correctly related to anything else.

**3. Reconstruction** — *"I can rebuild the relationships between the parts."*
Week 3's definition, unchanged: naming the parts, placing them relative to one
another, and giving the causal steps that make the thing work, without the
source in view. A reconstruction needs recall to supply its pieces, but recall
alone does not reach this rung — a list of correct terms with nothing joining
them is Recall, not Reconstruction.

**4. Explanation** — *"I can make this intelligible to someone else."* The
person produces the structure for an audience: recast, ordered, and pitched so
that another person — not just the explainer — could follow it. This is a
different demand from Reconstruction, which only has to satisfy the person
producing it.

**5. Justification** — *"I can support the claims inside my explanation."* Not
only asserting a causal step, but giving the reason that step holds — the
"because" a listener could doubt and ask about. An explanation can be complete
and still stop short of this: it can state that something happens without
defending why it must.

**6. Challenge** — *"I can respond when someone attacks the assumptions,
evidence or relationships in my explanation."* Someone else raises a specific
objection or counterexample, and the person answers it — by defending the
original account, revising it, or showing why the objection misses. This rung
cannot be produced by a person working alone; it requires another party to
supply the attack.

**7. Boundary** — *"I know where my explanation stops working."* The person
names, unprompted, a condition under which their own account would fail,
become uncertain, or need to be checked — the edge of their own model, found by
looking rather than told by someone else.

<p className="source-note">The seven rungs are this course's own construction, built for this course's question. They are not drawn from Bloom's taxonomy or from any other published scale, and they are not claimed to be one.</p>

## 4. Two things the ladder is not

It is not strictly ordered. A performance can show Boundary-level awareness
("I'm not sure this would survive being checked") without ever cleanly showing
Justification — section 6 has an example. And it is not a checklist every task
must complete: a short technical question may have no occasion for a Challenge
or a Boundary at all, and that is not a failure of the person answering it.
Treat the rungs as seven different *kinds* of evidence a performance can supply,
not seven steps of a staircase everyone must climb in order.

It is also not settled. Later weeks attack the assumptions built into it — this
week names one of those assumptions in section 8, and Week 12 returns to attack
the ladder itself, once you have used it for a term.

## 5. Pause and classify · 8 minutes

You are the examiner again. Below are five performances, all from earlier weeks,
quoted exactly as they appeared. For each, write down: which rung it reaches,
and one sentence defending that choice against an obvious objection. Attempt all
five before reading section 6.

**A.** Sam, Week 1, reading the reference with it open: *"Wi-Fi and internet
access are different."*

**B.** Theo, Week 2, writing from memory with nothing in front of him: *"The
slider pulls the two sides of teeth together and they interlock like fingers.
Pulling it back down separates them again."*

**C.** Priya, Week 3, reconstructing the lock from memory: *"A door lock has a
barrel that turns when you put the right key in. Inside there are pins of
different heights. The key pushes the pins up to the right heights, and then the
barrel can turn and the door opens. A wrong key does not push them to the right
heights, so it will not turn."*

**D.** Rowan, Week 4, condition (b), explaining to a novice with the reference
closed: *"Every pin is a different length and every notch is a different depth.
The right key lifts each pin by exactly its own amount, so all of them come
flush with that join at the same moment."*

**E.** Rowan, Week 4, condition (c), predicting an unfamiliar case: *"I think it
does not matter that the other five are fine. One is enough. Because the barrel
is one piece... Would you even be able to tell this key from a totally wrong
one, from the outside? I do not think you would. That feels wrong to me but I
cannot see what would make it false."*

## 6. Worked response: five performances, five disagreements

**A. Sam** looks like Recognition, and a strict reading holds him there: the
source was open, and repeating a stated sentence with it in view does not yet
require retrieving anything unaided. But a looser reading calls it Recall,
because Sam produced the sentence rather than pointing at it. The disagreement
is genuine and it is exactly the kind the ladder is built to make visible:
notice that it turns entirely on whether "the source was available" changes
which rung applies, or only how much we trust reaching it. Hold that question;
section 8 comes back to it.

**B. Theo** was writing with nothing in front of him, for a lesson that assumed
a naive reader, so the task he attempted was Explanation. Whether his
*performance* reaches it is a separate question, and Week 2 already answered it
for us: his sentence names an outcome ("they interlock like fingers") without
the mechanism that produces it, and omits what holds one tooth to the next. A
generous reading still credits Recall — he retrieved real, correct material —
but the missing causal step is precisely what Explanation requires and Theo's
sentence does not supply. Fluent and confident is not the same test as complete.

**C. Priya** attempted Reconstruction — nothing was in front of her, and the
task asked her to rebuild relationships, not list terms. Her performance,
though, is thin at exactly the place Reconstruction is defined: four parts are
missing, and the one relation she does state ("right heights, so it turns") is a
rule with no mechanism behind it, which Week 3 called a missing causal step. A
reader who scores by task attempted places her at Reconstruction; a reader who
scores by what actually got reconstructed drops her toward Recall, since what
survives is closer to a remembered outcome than a rebuilt structure. Both
readings are defensible, and neither is obviously right.

**D. Rowan's (b)** is Explanation by task — a source-free account, pitched for
someone with no vocabulary for locks — and it is fluent, complete in outline,
and wrong in one place: the two-pin stack has collapsed into a single pin, which
Week 4 showed is not a slip of phrasing but a change to the mechanism itself,
since it removes a whole failure mode. So this performance shows more than
Theo's — it has structure, an audience, and independent phrasing — and it still
does not establish the mechanism correctly. Reaching a rung is not the same as
reaching it accurately; the ladder tells you what *kind* of evidence you have,
not whether that evidence is good news.

**E. Rowan's (c)** is the hardest of the five, on purpose. The middle sentence —
*"one is enough... because the barrel is one piece"* — gives a reason for a
claim, which is Justification: it says why, not just that. The closing
sentence — *"would you even be able to tell this key from a totally wrong one...
I cannot see what would make it false"* — is Boundary: Rowan is naming,
unprompted, the edge of what this performance can show from the outside. Notice
what is missing in between: nothing here is Challenge, because nobody attacked
Rowan's account and made them defend it — the hedged opening ("do you want me to
guess?") is not a challenge, it is uncertainty about the genre of the task. So
this one performance touches rungs 5 and 7 without ever passing through 6, which
is exactly the "not strictly ordered" claim in section 4, demonstrated rather
than asserted.

## 7. The rung nobody produced

Read back across all five. None of Sam, Theo, Priya or Rowan across all three
conditions ever answers an objection someone else raised. That is not a
coincidence of a small sample — across four weeks of this course, nobody has yet
been shown a counterargument to their own explanation and asked to respond.
Challenge cannot be produced by a person working alone, however hard they think
about their own account; it needs a second party who has not already agreed with
them. That is a real limit on what the last four weeks of evidence can tell you,
and it is also a preview: an instrument that only measures what a solo
performance can supply is missing a whole rung, and the course will need an
activity that specifically manufactures one.

## 8. Pause and write: produce a Challenge · 6 minutes

Take the explanation you wrote in the Week 2 or Week 3 investigation — your own
zip or lock account, or the second mechanism you tried in the solo route. If you
did not keep one, use Priya's reconstruction in section 5.

Here is an attack on it: *"Your account never says what happens if two parts
are both slightly wrong at once — does the mechanism fail twice as badly, or is
one wrong part already enough that a second one changes nothing?"*

Write your response in three or four sentences. You may revise your original
claim, defend it as it stands, or say precisely what you would need to check to
answer the attack. Attempt this before reading the worked response.

<details>
<summary>Worked response: one way to meet the Challenge</summary>

Using the lock: a correctly working key already has to get every stack level
with the shear line, and a wrong key fails as soon as *one* stack is off. So two
wrong stacks do not make the lock "twice as locked" — it was already fully
stopped by the first one, and the plug has no notion of "a little open." The
attack is answered by the same fact Week 4 already established: the plug is one
object, not five independent ones, so failures do not add up, they just
duplicate a verdict the first failure had already reached.

This is a Challenge-rung response only if it does real work against the
objection — restating your original explanation more firmly is not a response
to it. Notice, too, that this answer reuses reasoning from Rowan's condition
(c): once you have the "one object, not five" relation, several objections
answer themselves the same way. That reuse is not cheating; a good Justification
is often the raw material several Challenges are answered with.

</details>

## 9. The assumption the ladder hides

Look again at rungs 2 and 3. Recall is defined as producing material *"without
the source in front of me."* Reconstruction is defined as rebuilding
relationships *"without the source in view."* The absence of a resource is not
incidental to these two rungs — it is written into their definition. A
performance produced with a reference open cannot, by this wording, count as
Recall or Reconstruction at all, however accurate it is.

That is a real assumption, not a neutral description, and this week is not the
place to defend or attack it — only to name it plainly, the way Week 4 asked you
to name the rule behind your own verdicts. The assumption is: **a performance
produced without support is intrinsically stronger evidence than the same
performance produced with support.** Section 6's disagreement about Sam already
turned on exactly this: does the source's presence change what rung applies, or
only how confidently we can trust it? Next week attacks that assumption
directly, by holding the mechanism fixed and asking the sharper question:
does needing support show that you do not understand, or only that you were
allowed to use what was available?

is only doing its job when it gives two careful readers something specific to
disagree about.

## Reading with a purpose

**Primary empirical source, reused.** Rozenblit, L., and Keil, F. (2002). "The
misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth."
*Cognitive Science*, 26(5), 521–562.
[Full text from the authors' lab](https://cogdevlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/rozenblit%20&%20keil%20%202002.pdf).
Weeks 2 and 3 used this for the gap between a fluent explanation and a complete
one. This week's rungs 4 and 5 — Explanation and Justification — are one way of
making that same gap into two separately checkable claims rather than one vague
one. This is an empirical finding, not a theory, and Study 1 alone rests on
sixteen graduate students at one university.

**Theoretical argument, reused.** Clark, A., and Chalmers, D. (1998). "The
Extended Mind." *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.
[Read the authors' text](https://www.consc.net/papers/extended.html), sections 1
to 3. Section 9 above names an assumption the ladder makes about resources
without yet arguing it either way. Before Week 6, read (or reread) Clark and
Chalmers' case that an external resource can participate in thinking rather than
merely assist it, and ask which of the seven rungs, if any, that argument would
change.

Both readings are carried over because they do new work for this week's
question rather than being repeated out of habit; neither is required to
complete the lesson.

## What this leaves unresolved

The ladder makes disagreement inspectable — you can now say exactly which rung
you placed a performance at, and someone else can argue you out of it, which
was not possible in Week 4. It has not made judgement automatic, and section 9
found something sharper than an open question: a specific assumption sitting
inside the wording of two of its own rungs, unexamined so far. Apply the ladder
yourself in the [Week 5 investigation](/sessions/05-the-ladder/), which asks you
to classify performances of your own and produce the Challenge-rung response
this lesson could only demonstrate once. Next week uses a new courier scenario to compare what a person is allowed to
consult while producing an answer — testing directly whether needing support
is evidence against understanding, or evidence of nothing at all.
