## Where Week 8 left us

Week 8’s surprise question exposed a limit: the final answer cannot show what existed before the probe. This week adds a prior state to the ladder record. **Pre-commitment** means recording a prediction and reason before checking, then preserving them beside the revision. Our new fictional case changes the setting, while continuing the same question about evidence.

> What can we learn from what someone thought before they checked?

This is a self-guided lesson. Both these notes and the slide deck contain the
explanations you need; no presenter is assumed. Allow about 30 minutes, with
another 15–20 minutes for the investigation. Use paper or a text document for
your answers, and keep what you write — this week's point depends on
comparing an early answer with a later one. You need no gardening background.
The people and incidents below are fictional.

## What you will learn

By the end, you should be able to explain what a prior state is and why
recording one matters, describe why a person's memory of "what I already
suspected" is not reliable evidence once they know the answer, and explain
what a recorded prior claim can and cannot establish about someone's later,
more accurate performance.

## 1. A plant, and a note written before looking anything up

Marlow's tomato plant has yellowing leaves that curl inward at the edges.
Before consulting any reference, Marlow writes down a diagnosis and a
confidence level: "Probably underwatering — the soil looks dry near the top
and the leaves are curling like they're trying to hold onto moisture.
Confidence: medium." Only after writing this does Marlow open a gardening
reference.

Tomato leaves that yellow and curl can have more than one common cause: too
little water, too much water (which can drown the roots and cause rot), or a
nutrient shortfall. Each has a different fix, so the distinction matters in
practice. For this lesson, though, the horticulture is secondary. What
matters is what Marlow did *before* finding out which cause was correct.

## 2. Marlow revises

The reference Marlow reads explains that overwatered tomato leaves often curl
and yellow in a pattern that looks similar to underwatering at first glance,
but the soil stays wet rather than dry, and the lower stem may show soft,
dark patches from root rot. Marlow checks the soil again — it is wet two
centimetres down, not dry — and finds soft dark patches near the base of the
stem.

Marlow's revised note: "Root rot from overwatering, not underwatering. The
soil is wet, not dry — I misjudged that from the surface, which had dried
out faster than the soil below it. The stem patches confirm root damage."
This explanation is more accurate than the first. But we now have two notes,
written at two different times, for two different reasons — and that pairing
is the interesting part.

## 3. Naming a prior state

A **prior state** is a claim — a prediction, a diagnosis, an explanation —
that someone commits to and records *before* they consult a source, receive
feedback, or otherwise learn the answer. Marlow's first note is a prior state.
The revised note is not: it was written with the answer already partly
visible. Recording a prior state fixes what someone thought at that specific
point, before anything else could change it.

Why does this distinction matter enough to build a whole lesson around it?
Because the alternative — simply asking someone afterward what they thought
beforehand — turns out to be far less trustworthy than it sounds.

## 4. A study on exactly this problem

Psychologist Baruch Fischhoff (1975) ran an experiment addressing this
directly. Two groups of subjects read the same brief description of a real
but unfamiliar historical event, with four possible outcomes listed. A
"Before" group estimated how likely each outcome was, without knowing which
had actually occurred. An "After" group was told which outcome had
occurred, then asked to estimate — as if they did not know — how likely each
outcome had seemed *beforehand*.

Across 24 such comparisons (varying the event, the outcome reported, and the
subject group), the After group's estimate of the reported outcome's
likelihood was higher than the Before group's estimate every single time
(sign test, p < .001). The average increase was 10.8 percentage points, and
over 70% of individual After subjects rated the reported outcome as more
likely than the *average* Before subject had. Simply learning what happened
made people believe, in hindsight, that it had looked more likely all along
— even though nothing about the original description had changed.

The detail that matters most for this course is Fischhoff's further finding
that subjects were largely **unaware** this had happened to them. In
follow-up experiments, subjects who were told an outcome and then asked to
reconstruct their own earlier judgment — or to guess what someone without
outcome knowledge would say — still overestimated it, as though the
hindsight-shifted belief felt like it had been there from the start. This is
why asking someone afterward "did you already suspect it?" is weak evidence:
the person answering is not lying, but their memory of the earlier belief has
been quietly revised by knowing the outcome.

## 5. Back to Marlow

Marlow's case is fictional and far lower-stakes than Fischhoff's historical
judgments, but the same mechanism threatens it. If Marlow had not written the
first note down, and we asked afterward "did you think it might be
overwatering?", Marlow's sincere answer would carry exactly the risk the study
describes — it might report a belief shaped by already knowing the answer,
not the belief that existed before. Because Marlow wrote the note down *before*
reading the reference, we have something a recalled version could never
give us: an unedited record of the reasoning available at that point.

Placed side by side, the two notes show something a single final answer
cannot. The final answer — "root rot from overwatering" — is correct and
well-supported, but on its own it gives us no way to tell whether Marlow
reasoned toward it, was told it outright, or copied it from somewhere. The
prior note supplies exactly that missing piece: independent reasoning from
visible cues, wrong about the cause but not unreasonable given what was
visible at the time. **A wrong first answer, honestly recorded, is still
evidence** — of what someone could infer from limited information, at a
specific moment, before help arrived.

## 6. Pause and write: a second case, before you look anything up

Allow four minutes. Marlow has a second plant: basil, growing on a sunny
windowsill, wilting badly by mid-afternoon during a heatwave. Before reading
further, write down what you think is wrong, why, and how confident you are
(low / medium / high). Do not revise this note once written — you will
compare it against a later answer.

<details>
<summary>A reference for the basil case, read after your prediction</summary>

Wilting that appears only in the hottest part of the day and largely
recovers by evening is often **heat stress**: the plant is losing water
through its leaves faster than its roots can replace it, even when the soil
has enough moisture. Wilting that persists into the next morning more often
points to a genuine water shortfall or root damage. Check the basil each
morning for two days (or imagine doing so, if you do not have a real plant)
and see whether it has recovered.

</details>

## 7. Worked response: revising the basil diagnosis

Suppose the basil looks wilted at 3pm but has fully recovered by the next
morning, two days running. That pattern matches heat stress rather than
underwatering: a genuinely dry plant would not fully recover overnight
without being watered. If your prior note guessed "underwatering," that
guess was reasonable — the two look similar at first — but the
overnight-recovery pattern is what distinguishes them. If your prior note
guessed "heat stress," you had that reasoning available before checking;
record that plainly rather than downplaying it.

Whichever way your prior guess landed, you now have a recorded prediction and
a recorded revision, written at genuinely different times with genuinely
different information available. That pairing, not just the final
correct-sounding answer, is the useful record. One instance does not
establish that you reliably reason well under time pressure or with a real
plant in front of you — it is a starting point, not a pattern.

## 8. A trap to watch for

Once you have read the worked response, the heat-stress explanation will
start to feel obvious. Resist rewriting your memory of your own prior note.
If it said "underwatering," that is what you thought at the time — leave it
exactly as written, the way Fischhoff's subjects could not stop their own
memories from doing invisibly. This is not about admitting fault. It is
about keeping the record honest enough to be useful later, including to you.

## 9. Calibration needs a prior claim to exist at all

**Calibration** describes how well someone's stated confidence matches their
actual accuracy — for example, whether the things a person calls "high
confidence" turn out right more often than the things they call "low
confidence." You cannot measure calibration from a single case, and you
cannot measure it at all from an answer given after the outcome is known,
because by then confidence and accuracy have collapsed into the same moment.
A recorded prior claim with a stated confidence level is the minimum unit
that calibration would need — repeated across many cases — to say anything
at all. This lesson gives you one such unit, not a calibration score.

## 10. Keep a short evidence record

Write four short entries about the basil case:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Prior claim | What you predicted, and your stated confidence. |
| What you consulted, and when | Before or after the prior claim was written. |
| Revised claim | What you concluded after checking, and what specific evidence changed your mind. |
| Limit | What this one instance does, and does not, establish about your reasoning generally. |

This follows the same observation / interpretation / limit habit introduced
in Week 1, applied here to a claim made before evidence rather than a claim
made after it.

## 11. What a prior state does not settle

A recorded prior state is real progress: it rules out the specific
distortion Fischhoff's subjects showed, because the claim exists in writing
from before the answer was known. But it does not tell us **how the
revision happened**. Did Marlow reason it out alone from the reference? Did a
friend point out the stem patches? Did an AI system suggest root rot
directly, and Marlow simply confirmed it? The prior-state record separates
"before" from "after." It cannot yet tell us whose reasoning produced the
"after."

## What this leaves unresolved

A prior state, honestly recorded, separates what existed before an answer
was known from what came after. It rules out the specific hindsight
distortion this lecture describes. It does not tell us how much of a
person's later, better answer came from their own reasoning versus
assistance available during the task — another person, a search, or an AI
system working alongside them. Next week takes that question on directly,
using a collaborator present *during* the task rather than only a reference
checked afterward.
