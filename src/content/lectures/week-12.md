## What makes the inference defensible?

**Validity** concerns how well the evidence supports the particular interpretation and use we propose. A successful performance does not validate every claim about the person. This returns to Week 1’s distinction between an observation and an inference. Farhan is a new fictional hard case; compare it with your own Week 11 record, using the supplied fallback if needed.

> What can we responsibly claim from the evidence we collected?

This is a self-guided lesson, and the course's last one. These notes and the
[slide deck](/decks/week-12/) each contain the explanations you need; no
presenter is assumed. Allow about 40 minutes, with another 20 if you complete
the [Week 12 investigation](/sessions/12-defending-the-inference/). You need
nothing open from earlier weeks, but this lesson reuses the seven-rung ladder
built in Week 5 without redefining it from scratch, so it helps to have read
that week once. Farhan is a new fictional examinee, distinct from Sam, Nadia,
Theo, Priya, Rowan and Elin.

This lesson does not add an eighth rung to the ladder, and it does not resolve
its own hard case cleanly by the end. That is the intended ending of the
course, not an unfinished one.

## What you will learn

By the end you should be able to state why a performance that passes every
rung of an instrument still does not settle what produced it; distinguish
**useful** evidence from **sufficient** evidence for a specific claim; name a
false positive and a false negative the same instrument can produce; explain
why adding another rung does not fix the underlying limit; and write a claim
about your own evidence that states exactly what it supports and what it does
not.

## 1. Where the course has got to

Weeks 1 to 4 built reasons to distrust an answer taken at face value: an
observation is not an inference, a fluent explanation can conceal missing
structure, a reconstruction can still be rehearsed, and two careful readers
judging the same texts under different conditions can reach opposite verdicts
with no way to show each other where they disagree. Week 4 closed by naming
what was missing — not more evidence, but a stated rule for weighing evidence
that is already in hand.

Week 5 built that rule: the seven-rung ladder, defined by what a performance
has to *do* rather than by how confident or fluent it sounds. Recognition,
Recall, Reconstruction, Explanation, Justification, Challenge, Boundary — each
rung is an operational definition, quoted in full in Week 5, that lets two
readers point at a specific rung and argue about it instead of trading
impressions. Weeks 6 to 11 spent six weeks testing that instrument against
harder conditions: what someone is permitted to consult, a changed form of the
same idea, an unannounced follow-up, a recorded prior state, a collaborator's
participation, and a real administered exam. The ladder survived all of it.
This week asks what surviving all of it actually proves.

## 2. Meet Farhan, and the topic

Farhan is a fictional examinee. You are his examiner, following the
administration standard this course has been arguing for since Week 6 and
Week 11: a real exam, on a declared topic, under stated conditions, with a
real live follow-up rather than a scripted one.

The topic is why the sky is blue during the day, and red or orange at sunset.
Sunlight reaching the atmosphere is scattered in all directions by the gases
in the air. Blue light, travelling as a shorter wave, scatters more than the
longer-waved colours, so during the day scattered blue light reaches your eyes
from all across the sky rather than only from the sun's direction. Near
sunset, sunlight has to travel through far more atmosphere to reach an
observer; even more of the blue gets scattered away over that longer path,
leaving mostly the reds and yellows to arrive directly.

Technical background: [NASA Space Place, "Why is the sky
blue?"](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/). This grounds the worked
example; it is not evidence about understanding or cognition, and the claim
this lesson makes is no broader than what that page states.

Farhan is told the topic in advance — "something about the sky's colour" —
but not the exact questions. This is the notice distinction Week 8
introduced: full notice of the topic, no notice of the specific follow-up.
Notes are removed before the exam begins, and no device is visible in the
room. Nothing about the administration is careless. That will matter later.

## 3. Farhan's performance, rung by rung

With the explanation briefly shown to him, Farhan repeats it accurately —
Recognition. A few minutes later, with the notes removed, he retrieves the
same content unprompted: "Blue light scatters more because it's a shorter
wave, and that happens in all directions, all over the sky." That is Recall:
produced from memory, nothing to consult.

Asked to rebuild the whole mechanism without being shown it again, Farhan
does: "Sunlight hits the air, the gas molecules scatter the short blue
wavelengths much more than the long red ones, and that scattered blue is what
reaches you from every direction — that's why the sky looks blue instead of
the sun looking blue and everything else looking normal." The parts are
named, related to one another, and the causal step is stated: Reconstruction.
Asked to explain the same idea to someone with no background, he recasts it
with an analogy about light bouncing off small obstacles far more often than
it travels straight through — pitched for a listener rather than only for
himself. That is Explanation, a different demand from Reconstruction because
it has to satisfy someone else.

Pressed on why shorter wavelengths would scatter more than longer ones,
Farhan answers: "The explanation I read said it's because they're shorter,
smaller waves — I don't know the physics much deeper than that, but that's
the stated reason, not just a fact I'm repeating." This is a defensible
Justification: he gives the reason inside the explanation rather than only
asserting the outcome, while also being honest about the limited depth of his
own grounding — a detail this lesson returns to.

## 4. Rung 6: an objection nobody scripted

You put one objection to Farhan live, unrehearsed on your side as well as
his: "Isn't that a contradiction? The daytime story says blue light reaches
you *because* it scatters. The sunset story says blue light is *lost*
because it scatters. Which is it?"

<summary>Compare after attempting your own version of this objection</summary>

Farhan answers without hesitating: "No — scattering happens the whole way, in
every direction, the whole time. At midday the path through the air is
short, so enough of that scattered blue still reaches you from all around.
At sunset the path is much longer, so the blue gets scattered again and
again before it ever gets to you — by the time it would reach your eyes,
it's already gone somewhere else, and what's left is mostly red and yellow."

This response does real work. It does not retreat to restating the original
explanation more firmly — which Week 5 already ruled out as a genuine
Challenge-rung response — and it resolves the apparent contradiction using
only the two facts already established: scattering happens in every
direction, and sunset light travels a longer path. Nothing new is smuggled
in to rescue the account.

</details>

Nobody scripted this objection for Farhan in advance, and his response meets
Week 5's Challenge definition on its own terms: he defends the original
account by showing why the objection misses, rather than revising it or
dodging it. This is genuinely rare evidence — Week 5 named Challenge as the
one rung nobody in the course's first four weeks had ever produced, precisely
because it needs a second party who has not already agreed with you.

## 5. Rung 7: a limit, volunteered

Without being asked, Farhan adds one more sentence: "I should say — I don't
actually know why sunsets are sometimes more orange than red, or whether
smoke or dust changes it. I'm confident in the scattering explanation itself,
but not in the finer detail past that."

Nobody asked him to name a limit. He found the edge of his own account and
said so unprompted — exactly Week 5's definition of Boundary. By the ladder's
own rules, Farhan has now passed all seven rungs: Recognition, Recall,
Reconstruction, Explanation, Justification, Challenge, Boundary, live, under
real questioning, from a real second person, with nothing to consult. On
paper this is the strongest single performance the course has examined.

## 6. Pause and write: your verdict · 4 minutes

Before reading further, write your own answer: does a clean pass on all
seven rungs, administered this carefully, justify concluding that Farhan
understands why the sky is blue and why sunsets are red? Write two or three
sentences, and name the one piece of evidence your verdict depends on most.

Attempt this before continuing. The next section does not resolve it for
you.

## 7. What the examiner learns afterward

A friend of Farhan's mentions, in passing, that the evening before the exam
Farhan spent over an hour in conversation with an AI assistant. Told only
that "something about light and colour" would be on the exam, he worked
through the daytime and sunset explanations, rehearsed a response to "isn't
that a contradiction," and was told that if pressed on the finer detail, it
was fine to say plainly that he didn't know it.

Nothing about the exam's administration was at fault here — no device was
visible, no notes were present, the follow-up was genuinely unscripted on
your side. This new information arrives after the fact, second-hand, from
someone who was not in the room, and is itself imperfect evidence. It does
not prove that Farhan's live answers were recited rather than reasoned. It
does mean you can no longer rule that out.

## 8. Why this reopens the case rather than closing it

Week 5 named an assumption sitting inside the ladder's own wording: Recall is
defined as producing material "without the source in front of me," and
Reconstruction as rebuilding relationships "without the source in view." Both
definitions check for a source being absent **at the moment of the
performance**. Neither asks where the content itself came from before that
moment.

A well-rehearsed answer, built collaboratively with an AI the night before
and retrieved from memory during the exam, satisfies "produced without the
source in front of me" exactly as well as an answer Farhan worked out
entirely alone. The two histories are not distinguishable from the
transcript, because the ladder was never built to distinguish them — it
was built to tell a live performance apart from one produced while reading a
source in the room, and Farhan's device was never in the room. A rehearsed
script recalled from memory is not the situation any of the seven rungs were
written to catch.

## 9. Two verdicts, both defensible, neither final

**Verdict A: count the pass.** The exam was administered correctly, on the
standard the course has argued for since Week 11. Some preparation before a
declared exam is normal and expected — you cannot penalise Farhan simply for
having studied the announced topic the night before. Record the pass, with
the preparation condition noted honestly rather than omitted.

**Verdict B: mark it inconclusive.** The specific objection Farhan handled so
cleanly may be one he had already rehearsed an answer to, which would make it
far weaker evidence of live reasoning than it appeared to be at the time.
Without a topic, or an objection, that Farhan genuinely could not have
anticipated, this particular exam cannot rule that possibility out.

Neither verdict is obviously the mistaken one. Holding both, and being
explicit about which piece of evidence would move you from one to the other,
is itself the disciplined move this lesson is asking you to practise.

## 10. Useful evidence is not the same as sufficient evidence

Farhan's performance is **useful** evidence of something real: under live
questioning, from a real examiner, with nothing to consult, he produced a
structurally complete and accurate account of this topic and responded
coherently to a genuine, unscripted challenge. That is not nothing, and
Verdict A is not a naive reading for treating it as real evidence of
capability.

It is not **sufficient** evidence that the understanding behind it is
independently Farhan's, because the identical transcript is equally
consistent with a well-prepared recollection of material substantially built
with a collaborator. The instrument cannot tell, from the transcript alone,
which explanation is correct — and no amount of additional polish in the
performance closes that gap, because polish is exactly what a good rehearsal
also produces.

This distinction — useful versus sufficient — is the sharpest single idea
this course can leave you with. An instrument can supply real, usable
evidence for a specific, narrow claim while still falling short of a much
larger claim the same evidence is often mistaken for supporting.

## 11. Two ways this could mislead you

A **false positive**: concluding "Farhan understands" from a performance that
was substantially a rehearsed, AI-co-produced script, recalled convincingly
under the pressure of a real exam. This is the failure mode this lesson has
walked through in detail.

A **false negative** is worth naming as its flip side, so the lesson does not
read as one-sided suspicion of every strong performance: someone who
understands the material deeply but freezes, rushes, or explains it badly
under live questioning could score lower on this same exam than Farhan did.
An instrument built to catch rehearsed fluency can just as easily undersell
real understanding that had every chance to show itself and didn't, on the
day, in that room.

## 12. Why one more rung will not fix this

It is tempting to propose an eighth rung: "explain something you could not
possibly have rehearsed." This moves the problem rather than solving it.
However carefully a genuinely novel topic is chosen, the examiner is still
trusting that no collaborator helped Farhan with it either — live, off
camera, or through preparation nobody in the room has any way to inspect.

Every rung this course has built converts an observed performance into a
claim about what produced it. No rung, and no achievable number of additional
rungs, can make that conversion certain, because the thing being inferred —
what Farhan actually understands, independent of any collaborator — is never
the thing directly observed. What is observed is always a performance; what
is claimed is always more than the performance strictly shows. That gap is
not a flaw in this particular ladder. It is the condition any instrument like
it works under.

## 13. Pause and write: defend your own inference · 6 minutes

Take a real piece of evidence from earlier in this course: your own Week 11
examination, a Week 5 or Week 9 record, or any performance you produced and
kept. Write one sentence claiming what it justifies, and a second sentence
stating what it does not — matching Verdict A and Verdict B's shape above,
applied to your own case rather than Farhan's.

<details>
<summary>Worked response: one way to write a defensible claim</summary>

"My Week 11 examination shows that, under live questioning with sources
removed, I could explain [topic] and respond to one unforeseen objection. It
does not show that I could do this on a topic I had no chance to prepare
for, or that my Challenge-rung response was not partly something I had
already rehearsed for a different reason."

Notice what this claim does: it names the exact condition the evidence was
produced under, and it names the exact thing that condition cannot rule out.
Neither sentence is a hedge added out of caution — each is doing real
argumentative work, in the same shape as Verdict A and Verdict B above.

A claim of this shape is not "I'm not completely sure, but I think I
understand it," which does not say what is uncertain or why. Nor is it
silence — refusing to conclude anything from a strong performance because it
*might* have been rehearsed throws away real evidence along with the
uncertain part of it.

</details>

## 14. What this course leaves you with

Twelve weeks have not produced an instrument that turns a performance into a
certainty about the person who produced it. They have produced something
narrower, and more genuinely useful: a way to state exactly what a piece of
evidence supports, what it does not, and why — so that a conclusion can be
checked, argued with by someone else pointing at the same rung, and revised
when better evidence arrives, instead of simply believed because it sounded
convincing.

That is what defending an inference means in this course's sense. Not
proving understanding. Defending a specific, bounded claim about what you
actually observed, and being honest about the gap between that claim and the
larger one it is often mistaken for.

## Reading with a purpose

**Booth, W. C., Colomb, G. G., Williams, J. M., Bizup, J., and FitzGerald,
W. T.** *The Craft of Research* (University of Chicago Press). Part 3,
"Making an Argument," covers claims, reasons and evidence, with dedicated
sections on warrants and on acknowledging and responding to objections. This
lesson uses the book at that general, structural level — a claim needs a
stated warrant and an honest qualification of what it does and does not
establish — which is exactly the shape of Verdict A and Verdict B above. This
citation is verified at the level of the book's public table of contents,
not from a page-level reading of the text; no page number or direct
quotation is claimed here, and none should be added without reading the
relevant pages directly.

**Rozenblit and Keil (2002)**, reused a final time from Weeks 2, 3, 5 and 8.
[Full text from the authors'
lab](https://cogdevlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/rozenblit%20&%20keil%20%202002.pdf).
Their diagnostic-question method — asking someone to explain past the point
where fluency runs out — is one concrete way to generate a genuine Challenge
like the one Farhan faced. This is an empirical finding about explanatory
depth, not a study of AI-assisted rehearsal; it supports the shape of the
Challenge rung, not the specific hard case built around it.

## What this leaves unresolved

Farhan's case is not decided, on purpose. Verdict A and Verdict B both remain
live at the end of this lesson, and choosing between them would require
evidence this exam was never designed to produce. What the course leaves you
with instead is a way to say precisely what you can and cannot claim from
what you have — which is a smaller thing than certainty, and a more honest
one. There is no Week 13. This is where the course's argument ends: not with
a perfect test, but with a disciplined limit on what any test, however well
built, is entitled to conclude.
