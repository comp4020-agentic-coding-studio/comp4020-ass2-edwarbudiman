> What becomes visible when the answer is no longer in front of us?

This is a self-guided lesson. These notes and the [slide deck](/decks/week-03/)
each contain everything needed; no presenter is assumed. Allow about 40 minutes,
plus 25 more for the [Week 3 investigation](/sessions/03-reconstruction/). You
need something to write with and nothing else: no lock, no tools, no paid book
and no AI account. Priya and the written answers below are fictional teaching
examples.

## What you will learn

By the end you should be able to distinguish consumption from reconstruction and
recall from reconstruction; to run a source removal on yourself and annotate what
came out using a four-part scheme; to read that annotation by the *type* of loss
rather than its volume; and to state precisely what a strong reconstruction does
and does not establish. You should also be able to say why an unwitnessed,
self-administered removal limits the claim you can make from it.

## 1. What Week 1 settled, in a sentence

Week 1 established the **examiner** — the person deciding what a performance
shows — and separated what you observed from what you inferred, insisted that
the conditions an answer was produced under belong in the record, and used Clark
and Chalmers to argue that working with a reference does not automatically
disqualify a performance. It also warned that taking a source away adds a demand
on memory, so you can end up testing something other than what you intended.
Week 2 found that a fluent explanation can still conceal missing parts and
causes.

This week does not re-argue any of that. Removal has a cost, and it has to be
chosen deliberately; that is settled. The question here is what the removal
**makes visible** and how an examiner reads the result. If the source is gone and
something is missing from what a person produces, that absence is evidence. Our
subject is what kind of evidence it is.

## 2. Consumption and reconstruction

Imagine Priya opening a short explanation of how a door lock works. It makes
sense as she goes; each sentence follows from the last; by the end she would say
she understands it. Call that **consumption**: taking in an explanation while it
is in front of you, following it, and finding it coherent.

Consumption is not a lesser activity, and this lesson is not an argument against
it. You cannot rebuild a structure you have never met, so consumption is where
the material comes from.

Now Priya puts the explanation away and writes the mechanism out with nothing in
front of her. **Reconstruction**, as this course uses the word, means rebuilding
the *structure* of an explanation without the source in view: naming the parts,
saying where each sits relative to the others, and giving the causal steps that
make the thing work. It is not reproducing the wording. A reconstruction in
completely different words, with the structure intact, is a success.

**Recall** and reconstruction are related but not interchangeable. Recall returns
remembered material — a term, a sentence, a list — and succeeds when the material
comes back. Reconstruction succeeds when the parts are present, placed correctly,
and joined by steps that carry the mechanism. A reconstruction usually needs
recall to supply its pieces, which is one reason a poor reconstruction is
ambiguous evidence: it may indicate a missing structure or a missing word.

## 3. Why a pin tumbler lock

A reconstruction can only be diagnosed if the thing reconstructed has nameable
parts. Then a gap is a specific missing item rather than a vague sense that an
answer was thin. A **pin tumbler lock** — the ordinary key lock on a front door —
has eight nameable parts, one named boundary and three states, and needs no prior knowledge.
You will not learn to open one; we are examining an explanation, not operating a
mechanism.

One warning before the reference. Rozenblit and Keil used a cylinder lock as a
test item and asked participants to explain step by step how one could pick one.
As in Week 2, this was a **diagnostic question**, a probe for a specific gap; here it informed self-assessment: their interest was in
what people discovered about their own knowledge, not in the technique. This
lesson does not cover picking a lock and nothing here should be read as
instruction in it.

## 4. The reference

Read this once or twice, then close it. Everything after it assumes you are not
looking at it.

An outer **casing** has a cylindrical hole in it. Inside that hole sits a
rotating cylinder called the **plug**. To unlock the door, the plug has to turn.
The front face of the plug carries a straight slot, the **keyway**, which the key
enters. At the back of the plug is a **cam**: a projecting piece that, as the plug
turns, draws back the **bolt**, the metal bar holding the door shut.

Five or six holes are drilled down through the plug. Each holds a **key pin**,
cut to its own length and rounded underneath so a key can slide across it.
Directly above each key pin, in a shaft in the casing, sits a **driver pin**,
with a **spring** above it pressing down. A key pin and the driver pin above it
make up one **stack**. The boundary where the plug meets the casing is the
**shear line**: the surface along which the plug has to rotate.

With **no key**, the springs push each stack down, so every driver pin is forced
part-way out of the casing and into the plug. Pins cross the boundary, and the
plug cannot turn. With the **correct key**, its cut edge lifts each key pin by a
different, exact amount, so every key-pin/driver-pin junction comes level with
the shear line. Nothing crosses the boundary, the plug turns, and the cam draws
the bolt back. With a **wrong key**, the cuts lift the stacks by the wrong
amounts: lifted too little, a driver pin still crosses into the plug; lifted too
far, a key pin is pushed up into the casing. One wrong stack is enough to hold
the plug.

Technical grounding: [Wikipedia’s article on the pin tumbler lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock).
That article names the boundary the **shear point**; “shear line,” used here,
appears in the same article and means the same surface. The article grounds the
mechanism and is not evidence for any claim about learning or cognition.

## 5. Pause and reconstruct · 8 minutes

Stop reading here and close this page, or cover section 4.
Write out how a pin tumbler lock works, from memory, in your own words. Name the
parts; say where each sits relative to the others; explain why the plug cannot
turn with no key, why it turns with the correct key, and what a wrong key does.
Permitted: your memory and something to write with, nothing else. Your output is
one paragraph or a labelled list, which you keep. The purpose is to produce the
artefact the rest of this lesson examines.

### The honest problem with that instruction

You can scroll back up. Nothing prevents it, and nobody is watching you. A source
removal you administer to yourself is not something another person can check.

Follow the instruction anyway, for a practical reason rather than a moral one:
your reconstruction is the raw material for the next step, and a copied version
gives that step nothing to work on. You would lose the lesson rather than fail a
test. And if you did look back, write that down. “Reconstructed with one look at
the reference” is a usable record; an unrecorded look is what ruins it.

Treat that unverifiability as material to study, not a flaw to paper over.
Examining yourself means holding both roles at once: you are the person producing
the performance and the examiner judging it, and you cannot independently confirm
your own account of the conditions. That does not make self-examination
worthless. It makes the condition — self-administered, unwitnessed — part of the
record, exactly as Week 1 required. State it, and claim less than you otherwise
would.

## 6. Restore the source and annotate · 5 minutes

Read section 4 beside your own version and mark every
difference on your reconstruction with one of four letters.

| Letter | What it marks |
| --- | --- |
| **P** | A **part** omitted: a named component missing altogether. |
| **R** | A **relation** omitted: parts named, but not placed with respect to one another. |
| **C** | A **causal step** omitted: a “because” the mechanism needs. |
| **X** | A **scenario** omitted: a case the reference covered, such as the wrong key. |

Your output is the annotated reconstruction with a count for each letter. The
scheme exists so that “something was lost” becomes a specific, arguable finding.

A written model answer, not a record of anybody’s work:

> “An outer casing holds a rotating plug. The key goes into the keyway at the
> front of the plug, and a cam at the back pulls the bolt. Six holes run down the
> plug, each holding a key pin cut to its own length. Above each key pin, in the
> casing, sits a driver pin with a spring pressing down. Plug and casing meet at
> the shear line. With no key, the springs push the stacks down, driver pins cross
> into the plug, and it jams. A correct key raises every junction level with the
> shear line, so nothing crosses it, the plug turns, and the cam draws the bolt. A
> wrong key lifts a stack too little, leaving a driver pin in the plug, or too far,
> pushing a key pin up into the casing.”

This is strong. Every part appears, the stacking relation is right, and all three
states are explained with the boundary doing the causal work. It still loses
something: **C** — the reference said the correct key lifts each pin *by a
different, exact amount*, because the pins differ in length. This version says a
correct key raises every junction level with the shear line without saying that
differing cut depths on the key are what achieve it.

One **C** against an otherwise complete structure is a small and specific
finding, which is exactly what a scheme is for.

<details>
<summary>Priya’s reconstruction, and its annotation</summary>

> “A door lock has a barrel that turns when you put the right key in. Inside
> there are pins of different heights. The key pushes the pins up to the right
> heights, and then the barrel can turn and the door opens. A wrong key does not
> push them to the right heights, so it will not turn.”

Notice first that nothing in it is false. Every sentence is consistent with the
reference.

**P** — four parts are gone: the springs; the driver pins, since Priya has one
set of pins where the reference had two; the cam; and the bolt.

**R** — nothing is placed. There is a barrel, and pins are “inside,” but there is
no casing and nothing about driver pins sitting above key pins.

**C** — the largest loss. “Right heights, so it turns” is a rule, not a
mechanism. The shear line is gone, so nothing explains *why* correct heights
permit rotation.

**X** — the wrong key appears, but only as under-lifting. Over-lifting is absent.

## 7. Reading the annotated difference as evidence

Count the letters, then weigh them. Priya’s four **P** items and her one **C**
are not equivalent findings.

A missing part name is often a vocabulary gap. Priya may hold the structure and
lack the phrase “driver pin”; a follow-up in which she describes the same
arrangement in her own words would tell us. A missing causal step is a different
matter. Without the shear line there is no account of why the plug turns, so
“right heights, so it turns” could have been produced by a reader who never took
in the boundary at all. **A lost noun and a lost “because” are not the same
loss.** Priya can say what happens; she cannot yet say what makes it happen.

A missing **X** is the least conclusive of the four, because the reader may have
judged the wrong-key case not worth writing down rather than not having it. Where
that matters, ask for the scenario directly instead of inferring from its
absence.

## 8. Take a strong reconstruction seriously

**Allow three minutes.** Suppose a reader produces something as complete as the
model answer, with no letters to mark. Before reading on, write two things that
result would establish and two things it would not; “I might be wrong” is not one of them, because it
names no missing evidence.

It establishes that at that moment, with the reference out of view, this reader
produced a structure containing those parts, those relations, those causal steps
and the wrong-key condition. That is a real performance, and it is considerably
more than handing the reference’s own sentences back. It is also more inspectable
than a rating of confidence: there is a written artefact, and we can point at the
part of it we are discussing.

It does not establish four things:

- **Not that the structure was built rather than memorised.** A reader who
  learned the reference by heart yesterday hands in the same page.
- **Not that the reference was actually closed**, because nobody witnessed it.
- **Not that the same thing would happen for another mechanism**, or for one not
  studied minutes earlier.
- **Not that the reader can use the structure** on a question the reference did
  not answer — say, what a lock with one broken spring would do.

That is the week's limitation in full: reconstruction is stronger evidence, and
it can still be memorised, rehearsed or assisted, and in a self-guided setting
nobody can confirm the source was closed.

## 9. Reconstruction is an instrument, not a virtue

Reconstruction is stronger evidence than a fluent read-through for one stated
reason: the source is not supplying the structure. That is the whole claim. It
does not make reconstruction morally better than reading, and it does not make
consumption a bad habit. Priya could not have reconstructed anything she had
never read. Consumption is the scaffolding; reconstruction is a measuring
instrument built on top of it.

This matters for the course as much as for the reader. A lesson that treats
reconstruction as self-improvement has changed the subject from examining
understanding to recommending a study habit, and the recommendation would also be
false, because the instrument depends on the consumption it is supposed to
replace.

## 10. What the sources support, and what they do not

Rozenblit and Keil asked sixteen graduate students from various departments at
Yale to rate how well they understood each item on a list of 48 devices and
phenomena, using a 7-point scale on which 1 was the lowest level of understanding
and 7 the highest. Each participant then wrote a detailed, step-by-step causal
explanation of four test items — the set including a cylinder lock also included
a helicopter, a quartz watch and a sewing machine — and re-rated themselves.
Ratings dropped. A repeated-measures ANOVA across their five rating points gave
F(4, 56) = 16.195, p < .001, and the planned contrasts between the first rating
and each of the next three were significant at p < .002. Ratings rose again at
the final point, after participants had read an expert description, so they were
not simply losing confidence in everything.

Two things follow for us, and one does not. Being asked to produce the
explanation, rather than being handed one, is what moved the ratings; and the
authors’ own reading of the unchanged rating after the expert text is that the
diagnostic questions were not unfair, since participants still judged their lower
estimates accurate once they saw what an expert thought was essential. What does
*not* follow is any claim about what those participants could produce. Their
measurement is, as Week 2 established, a **self-rating**: a number a participant gives for their own
understanding. A drop in it shows that participants revised their opinion of
themselves.

Our annotated reconstruction is a step better, because the evidence is a page we
can inspect rather than a number. That improvement is our adaptation of their
design, not their finding. Running it once on yourself, with no control and no
comparison, replicates nothing.

The annotation step also exists because people differ widely in noticing that
they have not understood something. Chi, analysing students explaining physics
worked examples aloud, reports that poor learners acknowledged a comprehension
failure about 15% of the time while good learners did so about 46% of the time,
and that once a failure was detected it was usually followed by an attempt to
explain. Her students had the text in front of them, which is a different
condition from ours, so we take the point about noticing rather than the result
about self-explaining. Priya, mid-reconstruction, may simply not feel a gap where
there is one. Restoring the source removes the need to notice: the reference
finds the gap for you.

## 11. Keep a short record

Write five lines and keep them with your annotated page.

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Condition | “Self-administered and unwitnessed; reference closed,” or “one look back.” |
| Observation | “Without the reference, I produced…” |
| Counts | How many P, R, C and X. |
| Interpretation | “This supports the claim that I can…” |
| Limit | “It does not show that I can…” |

A useful limit names the evidence you are missing. “Explain a mechanism I had not
just read” is one; “I don’t really understand locks” is not.

An examiner’s record of Priya might read: *Condition* — self-administered,
unwitnessed, reference closed on her own account. *Observation* — she named a
rotating barrel and pins of differing heights, and stated that correct heights
permit rotation. *Counts* — P 4, R 2, C 1, X 1. *Interpretation* — she retained
the outcome of the mechanism and one of its components. *Limit* — the single C is
the finding that matters; nothing here shows she holds the boundary that makes
the outcome follow.

## Reading with a purpose

**Primary evidence:** Rozenblit, L., and Keil, F. (2002). “The misunderstood
limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth.” *Cognitive Science*,
26(5), 521–562.
[Full text from the authors’ lab](https://cogdevlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/rozenblit%20&%20keil%20%202002.pdf).
Read Study 1 and ask: of their five ratings, which one actually takes the source
away? This is an empirical finding, demonstrated across twelve studies in the one
paper, and Study 1 rests on sixteen graduate students at one university.

**A complementary argument:** Chi, M. T. H. (1998). “Self-explaining: the dual
processes of generating inferences and repairing mental models.” Online
manuscript prepared for R. Glaser (ed.), *Advances in Instructional Psychology*
(Lawrence Erlbaum), dated 15 September 1998.
[Manuscript PDF](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Self%20explanations/Self%20Explaining%20%28Chi%29.pdf).
Read “What self-explaining is not” and the section on self-repair, and ask how
much of a reconstruction is repair rather than retrieval. It supplies both
empirical findings and a theoretical argument; note that its own title page
describes it as in press, so the published chapter’s title and pagination may
differ.

**Carried from Week 1:** Clark, A., and Chalmers, D. (1998). “The Extended Mind.”
*Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19. [Read the paper](https://www.consc.net/papers/extended.html).
Week 1 asked when a resource belongs inside the activity. Ask something different
this week: if a resource can genuinely participate in thinking, what exactly are
we measuring when we take it away? This is a philosophical argument, not evidence
about reconstruction.

## What this leaves unresolved

One task cannot settle the question. Priya’s page could be thin because she does
not hold the mechanism, or because she writes tersely, or because eight minutes
was short, or because a written paragraph is not the form in which she thinks. A
single reconstruction cannot distinguish those, and the unwitnessed condition
leaves the strongest result open to a simple alternative explanation.

So the next move is to compare the same person’s performances across conditions
rather than trust one of them. Week 4 asks why the same person looks more or less
knowledgeable under different conditions, and reuses this lock under two or three
of them. Apply this week’s lesson first in the
[Week 3 investigation](/sessions/03-reconstruction/), which takes the same
protocol further and asks you to defend one judgement from what you actually
produced.
