## Where Week 6 left us

Week 6 changed the resources available during a performance. This week keeps attention on the idea but changes its form: audience, representation or problem. It tests a different ladder assumption: that an Explanation-rung response reflects an adaptable model rather than a rehearsed form. Our new fictional example supplies the background it needs.

> If you can explain an idea in one way, does that mean you understand it — or only that you can produce that one explanation?

This is a self-guided lesson. Both these notes and the slide deck contain the
explanations you need; no presenter is assumed. Allow about 35 minutes, with
another 20 minutes if you complete the full investigation. Use paper or a text
document for your answers. No finance background is required. Kavi, the
colleague, and every quoted response below are fictional.

## What you will learn

By the end, you should be able to explain what it means for a performance to
change **form** — audience, representation or problem, not just wording — and
why a different form can be stronger evidence of understanding than a repeated
one. You should also be able to name the limit of that idea: a change of form
can itself be rehearsed, so it raises the bar on a memorised answer without
removing the possibility of one.

This week follows directly from [Week 6](/lectures/week-06/), which sorted
resources into ones that function as part of a person's own competence and
ones consulted for the first time under examination — and closed by asking
whether someone can present the same idea in an unfamiliar form: a new
audience, a new representation, a task they were not drilled on. Week 6 asked
about **support**; this week asks about **task** — what you are asked to do
with an idea, once you have it, regardless of what you consulted to get there.

## 1. An explanation that already works

Kavi read a short explanation of compound interest — how savings or debt can
grow faster than a fixed yearly amount suggests — and wants to check what they
actually understand, not only what they can repeat.

**Compound interest** is interest calculated on the original amount *and* on
interest already added, not only on the original amount. The Federal Reserve
Bank of St. Louis describes it as interest “on your original principal — plus
on the interest your investment generates”: interest earning interest.

A small worked example makes the mechanism concrete. Put $1,000 in an account
paying 10% a year, compounded annually: $1,000 → $1,100 after year one, $1,100
→ $1,210 after year two, $1,210 → $1,331 after year three. Compare that with
**simple interest**, which adds a fixed $100 every year regardless of the
balance: $1,000 → $1,300 after three years. The extra $31 in the compound case
is interest earned on interest already added — the mechanism, not a rounding
error.

Technical grounding: [Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, “How Compound
Interest Works”](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2018/september/how-compound-interest-works)
(2018). This grounds the arithmetic only; it is not evidence for any claim
about learning or understanding.

## 2. What "a different form" means

A performance changes form when it addresses a different **audience** (a
child, a colleague), uses a different **representation** (a story, a written
justification, numbers), or answers a different **problem** posed from the
same idea (explain it, apply it, compare two cases, find an error). Two
performances can use identical vocabulary and still differ in form, if one
only has to be repeated and the other has to be built from the idea itself.

## 3. Kavi's first performance

Kavi's younger sibling asks how a savings account works. Kavi explains:
“Every year, the bank pays you interest. But next year, you also get paid
interest on that interest — so your money is a bit like a snowball. It picks
up more the further it rolls, even without you adding anything.” The sibling
nods and repeats the snowball line back. Kavi seems pleased.

### Pause and write · 3 minutes

Read Kavi's snowball explanation again. Is this strong evidence that Kavi
understands compound interest, rather than a comfortable, well-rehearsed line?
Write two sentences: what the explanation shows, and one thing it does not yet
show. Consider what else could produce the same fluent explanation.

<details>
<summary>Worked response: fluent is not the same as flexible</summary>

The explanation is accurate and clearly delivered, which is worth something —
a confused explanation would be worse evidence, not better. But it is also a
well-worn analogy that could have been read, memorised or reused nearly
unchanged. Week 2's illusion of explanatory depth showed that a fluent
explanation can still conceal what a person cannot yet do with an idea. If
Kavi's explanation only ever reappears in this one shape — same audience, same
story, same numbers — we cannot tell whether Kavi possesses the underlying
relationship or one rehearsed description of it.

</details>

## 4. A name for this: transfer

Educational psychologists call this **transfer**: using something learned in
one context to perform in a different one. Barnett and Ceci's review
distinguishes transfer to a *similar* context (**near transfer**) from
transfer to a genuinely *dissimilar* one (**far transfer**) — different in
domain, audience, format or the kind of problem asked. They argue that much of
the century-long disagreement over whether people "really" transfer what they
learn comes from treating all transfer as one thing, when studies differ
enormously in how far the new context sits from the original.

As an illustration, Barnett and Ceci report a study (Schliemann &amp;
Magalhães, 1990) of unschooled Brazilian cooks solving proportional-reasoning
problems — the same underlying mathematical relationship — across three
domains. The cooks performed almost perfectly on pricing problems, worse on
recipe problems, and worse again on medicine-dosage problems: an unfamiliar
domain, even though nothing about the maths itself had changed. The same
competence produced very different performance depending on how far the new
problem sat from familiar territory. That cuts both ways: a person who fails
an unfamiliar version has not necessarily failed to *understand* the
relationship — unfamiliarity with the new domain is doing some of the work
too. Changing the form is informative, but it is not a clean test on its own.

## 5. Back to Kavi: an unexpected question

A colleague overhears the snowball explanation and asks something different:
two loan offers quote the same 6% yearly rate — one compounds annually, the
other monthly. Which one will cost more in total interest, and why? This is
not a repeat of the sibling conversation. It changes the audience, drops the
analogy, and asks for a comparison rather than a description.

### Pause and write · 4 minutes

Before reading Kavi's answer, attempt the colleague's question yourself: will
monthly or annual compounding cost more at the same 6% nominal rate, and why?
Write your prediction and your reasoning in two or three sentences. You do not
need exact numbers, only the direction and the mechanism.

<details>
<summary>Kavi's answer: two versions, and which is correct</summary>

**Version A:** “Monthly compounding should cost less. Each month's interest is
worked out on a smaller amount, calculated more often, in smaller pieces — so
it adds up to less overall.”

**Version B:** “Monthly compounding should cost slightly more. Interest gets
added to the balance twelve times a year instead of once, so interest starts
earning interest sooner and more often — even though each individual addition
is smaller.”

Check it with numbers: on a $10,000 loan at 6% for three years, compounded
annually it grows to about $11,910; compounded monthly it grows to about
$11,967 — roughly $57 more. **Version B is correct.** Version A repeats the
surface feature of the sibling explanation — “interest earns interest” —
without following where that mechanism actually leads: more compounding
events, not smaller amounts, is what raises the total.

</details>

Version A is not confused about the definition; it can even quote the
snowball line correctly. What it has not done is use the underlying
relationship to reason about an unfamiliar comparison — closer to Barnett and
Ceci's fishermen, who could apply an intuitive method inside their trained
domain but not carry a school-taught procedure into a new one. Fluent
restatement and working transfer can come apart.

Version B's success is stronger evidence of a working model than the sibling
explanation alone: it reasons from the mechanism to a new, previously unseen
comparison and gets the right answer for the right reason. But one success in
one new form is still one data point. We do not yet know whether Kavi could
handle a third, different reformulation — or whether this exact comparison was
something Kavi had already seen.

## 6. The deeper problem with changing the form

Reformatting and adaptation can themselves be rehearsed. If loan-compounding
comparisons are a common example in whatever Kavi read, a correct answer here
might reflect a second memorised template rather than a freely applied
relationship — apparent transfer that actually rests on prior familiarity with
this specific kind of question, not on the underlying idea. From the
transcript alone, we cannot distinguish "Kavi reasoned from the mechanism to a
genuinely new case" from "Kavi had met a version of this comparison before."
Both would produce exactly Version B. Changing the form raises the bar on what
a rehearsed answer would need to cover; it does not remove the possibility of
rehearsal.

## 7. Apply the lesson yourself

Pick one thing you already feel you understand — anything, from your own
study or interests. Write a short explanation of it as you would to someone
with no background in the topic. Then write a second explanation of the
*same idea*, aimed at someone who would ask a harder, more technical question
about it — a critique, an application, or a comparison. Use a genuinely
different form, not just shorter words.

Compare your two explanations. Did the second one restate the first with
different vocabulary, or did it use the underlying idea to do something new —
justify, compare, predict, or defend against an objection? If the second
explanation could have been written by someone who only memorised the first,
the change of form has not yet told you anything new about your own
understanding.

Use [the Week 7 investigation](/sessions/07-change-the-form/) to carry out
this activity in full and keep a written record.

## 8. Keep a short record

Write three sentences: **Observation** — what did the two explanations
actually say? **Interpretation** — what does the harder-form version
specifically support, that the first version alone did not? **Limit** — what
would a third, still more different form need to show, that this pair does
not yet establish? This is the same evidence record used since Week 1;
audience and form are simply new conditions to record.

## Reading with a purpose

**Primary synthesis:** Barnett, S. M., and Ceci, S. J. (2002). “When and
Where Do We Apply What We Learn? A Taxonomy for Far Transfer.” *Psychological
Bulletin*, 128(4), 612–637.
[Read the paper](https://rapunselshair.pbworks.com/f/barnett_2002.pdf). Ask:
which of their nine dimensions changed between Kavi's two performances, and
which stayed the same? This is a synthesis of the transfer literature — a
review that classifies existing studies along shared dimensions — not a
single experiment, and its claim about far transfer is a pattern across
studies, not a settled effect size.

## What this leaves unresolved

Every task in this lesson — Kavi's, and your own — was still something you
knew to expect, inside a lesson built to prompt exactly this kind of answer.
If Kavi, or you, knew roughly what kind of question was coming, some of what
looked like flexible reasoning could still be a rehearsed *strategy* for
producing convincing-looking answers to unexpected-seeming questions, rather
than evidence about the specific case asked. Next week asks what changes when
the test is not merely unfamiliar in form, but genuinely unannounced — and
what a surprised response can show that a prepared one cannot.
