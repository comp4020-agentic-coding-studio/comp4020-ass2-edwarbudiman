> You found an explanation. How would you tell whether you understand it?

This is a self-guided lesson. Both these notes and the slide deck contain the
explanations you need; no presenter is assumed. Allow about 30 minutes, with
another 20 minutes if you complete the extended investigation. Use paper or a
text document for your answers. No networking background, working router, or AI
account is required. The people and incidents below are fictional.

## What you will learn

By the end, you should be able to separate an observation from an inference,
explain why a relevant answer can have more than one origin, and choose a
follow-up question that gives you more useful evidence. You should also be able
to explain why using a reference does not automatically disqualify a performance.

## 1. Connected to Wi-Fi, but websites will not load

Your laptop is connected to your home Wi-Fi. Yet a website will not load. You
search for an explanation and find: “A Wi-Fi connection does not guarantee
internet access.” The sentence sounds useful. But what would it mean to
understand it, rather than recognise or repeat it?

Start with the technical distinction. Wi-Fi provides a wireless connection to a
local network. At home, the Wi-Fi access point is often part of the router.
Internet access also requires a working route beyond that network. One part of
the journey can work while another fails. A website failing to load alone does
not identify the failed part.

For example, the home Wi-Fi equipment could remain powered on while an
internet-provider outage interrupts the onward connection. This explains how
local connectivity without internet access is possible. It does not diagnose
every situation in which a website fails to load.

Technical background: [Microsoft’s Wi-Fi guidance](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/connectivity-networking/fix-wi-fi-connection-issues-in-windows)
and [Cloudflare’s introduction to routers](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/network-layer/what-is-a-router/).
Our cases simplify the technology so we can focus on judging understanding.

## 2. A correct sentence is a starting point

Imagine Sam reads that explanation and says, “Wi-Fi and internet access are
different.” You are the examiner: the person deciding what this performance
shows. You might be a teacher, a colleague checking an explanation, or someone
examining your own learning.

An **observation** describes what happened: “Sam repeated the distinction while
the explanation was visible.” An **inference** is a conclusion drawn from it:
“Sam understands why local connectivity can work without internet access.” The
second statement goes beyond what was directly observed.

Why does this matter? Sam could understand the distinction and have consulted
the source to check the wording. Sam could also repeat it without understanding
what a local network is. Both explanations fit the observation. That uncertainty
does not establish that Sam lacks understanding; it means we need more evidence
to distinguish the possibilities.

**Familiarity** is the feeling that something is recognisable or easy to follow.
A clear explanation can feel convincing before you have tried using the idea.
*Make It Stick* supplies a useful caution about relying on that feeling. Our
application is to ask what someone can do with an explanation, while avoiding
the opposite mistake of treating successful recall as proof of understanding.

## 3. Pause and write: use the distinction

Allow two minutes. Imagine an internet-provider outage. The router and its Wi-Fi
access point remain on. Could your laptop still be connected to the home Wi-Fi?
Explain why in two sentences. You may consult the notes; record whether you did.

Yes. The laptop could retain its local wireless connection even though the
onward internet connection is interrupted. These are different parts of the
journey, so losing one does not necessarily remove the other.

The reasoning matters because “yes” alone leaves us unsure how the answer was
chosen. An answer that separates the two connections is more inspectable. If
you copied or adapted the explanation, that condition also belongs in the record.

## 4. Why keeping the source can be legitimate

If the question is “Can Sam explain this without a reference?”, then removing
the reference makes sense. But suppose the question is “Can Sam accurately
interpret a support article for someone else?” Consulting the article belongs
to that task. Removing it introduces a demand on memory and changes what is
being examined.

Clark and Chalmers’ *The Extended Mind* challenges the assumption that thinking
must be understood entirely within the individual. It argues that external
resources can participate in cognition. This philosophical argument gives us a
reason to examine the role of a resource, rather than reject it merely for
being external. It does not show that every assisted answer is competent.

For this course, record the task and its conditions before interpreting the
result. “Explained with notes available” is a useful description, not an apology.
Later weeks will examine the difficulties of separating a person’s contribution
from a collaborator’s, including AI. For now, preserve the distinction.

## 5. Choose a follow-up that answers your uncertainty

Suppose you want to know whether Sam can use the idea rather than repeat its
wording. Ask about a changed situation:

> Another device freshly loads the same website at the same time, using the
> same home Wi-Fi, with mobile data off. Does this prove the laptop’s hardware
> is broken?

**Pause for three minutes.** Give a reason and name one further observation you
would want. Keep the source available so that the task still concerns reasoning
with support, not unaided recall.

<details>
<summary>Worked response: what the new evidence establishes</summary>

No. The successful device shows that the website is reachable through this home
connection for at least one device at that time. It does not establish why the
laptop failed. Its browser or network configuration could be involved; hardware
failure is only one possibility.

Trying another browser on the laptop could provide additional evidence, but
would not settle every possibility. This is an example of reasoning from
limited information, not a complete troubleshooting guide.

</details>

The follow-up has a purpose: it asks whether Sam can limit a conclusion in light
of a new observation. A question containing harder networking vocabulary would
not necessarily help us distinguish comprehension from repetition.

A good response gives us more evidence, but the follow-up may also teach Sam
something. An explanation produced after discussion should not be treated as
proof that the same explanation existed before the discussion.

## 6. Keep the two questions separate

We have uncertainty **about the network**: what actually caused the failure?
We also have uncertainty **about Sam**: what does this response show about Sam’s
understanding? Naming a plausible fault is not the same as demonstrating the
reasoning that makes it plausible.

A careful examiner might record:

| Part of the record | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Observation | Sam rejected the hardware diagnosis and gave another possible explanation, while consulting the notes. |
| Interpretation | Sam used the supplied distinction to explain why this evidence does not identify a hardware fault. |
| Limit | This does not establish independent diagnosis; Sam may have developed the explanation during the probe. |

This record is our teaching tool, not a validated psychological scale. Its value
is in making your reasoning inspectable and leaving room for correction.

## 7. Apply the lesson yourself

Use [the Week 1 investigation](/sessions/01-getting-started/) to retain your own
answers and a short evidence record. Work alone or with a partner. The goal is
not to catch someone failing. If an answer is strong, say what it supports and
what further claim would still require evidence.

Before you finish, explain these distinctions in your own words:

- What you observed versus what you inferred.
- Using a reference competently versus demonstrating unaided recall.
- Finding a possible network explanation versus judging a person’s understanding.

If you cannot explain one yet, revisit that section and add an example. The
revised explanation is evidence of what you can do now; preserve the earlier
answer if you want to examine what changed.

## Reading with a purpose


**Book companion:** Brown, P. C., Roediger, H. L. III, and McDaniel, M. A. (2014).
*Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning*. Belknap Press of Harvard
University Press. Begin with Chapter 1, “Learning Is Misunderstood,” if you have
access. Ask: why might an experience that feels like learning give an unreliable
indication of what someone can later do?
[Author resources](https://www.makeitstick.com/) include
[the book’s endnotes](https://www.makeitstick.com/_files/ugd/99359a_b68cc695ab0b42cbaea9e8afe6d7e282.pdf).
Those are supporting references, not the chapter itself. Buying the book is not
required to complete this lecture.

**Primary argument:** Clark, A., and Chalmers, D. (1998). “The Extended Mind.”
*Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.
[Read the authors’ text](https://www.consc.net/papers/extended.html), sections 1–3.
Ask: when does a resource become part of doing the thinking? Bring one objection.
We use this paper because it challenges the assumption that relevant competence
must always reside inside an unaided individual.

## What this leaves unresolved

An accessible answer can be useful without settling what its user understands.
Describe how it was produced, consider alternative explanations, and choose a
follow-up that addresses the uncertainty. Next week asks whether fluent
explanation is enough—or whether it can also persuade an examiner too quickly.
