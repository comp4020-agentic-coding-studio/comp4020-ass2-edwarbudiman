## The brief

> Design a test for someone else's claim to understand something, run it on
> them, and then tell us what your test actually measured.

This is the course's secured, invigilated point: the administration happens
live, in front of a witness, because a transcript afterward can't settle
whether the exchange was real. Pick a peer, a claim they've made about
understanding something, and build a short instrument — drawing on the
seven-rung ladder from Week 5 — meant to distinguish real command of the
material from a fluent-sounding echo of it. Plant one deliberate trap: a
fake explanation, a subtly wrong premise, a question the rungs shouldn't be
able to answer. Then run it.

The interesting failure here is not "the instrument was too easy." It's an
instrument that returns a confident, wrong verdict — passing a fake, or
failing something real — because that's the same failure mode the fluent AI
answer in Week 12 will exploit.

## What you submit

The instrument (questions, rungs targeted, what a pass and a fail look like),
a record of the live administration, and an honest report of what the trap
revealed. Name explicitly what a reader should *not* conclude from your
result — what this particular instrument cannot rule out.
