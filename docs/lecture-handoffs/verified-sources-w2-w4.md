### Study 1 procedure — verified detail

Use these facts; do not embellish them.

- 16 graduate students from various departments at Yale University.
- A **7-point scale** of understanding. Verbatim from the paper: the point
  "1" was the lowest level of understanding and "7" the highest. Participants
  were first trained on the scale using two worked examples — a **cross bow**
  (most adults have a good mechanistic understanding) and a **global
  positioning satellite receiver** (most do not).
- The eight test items were: a speedometer, **a zipper**, a piano key, a flush
  toilet, **a cylinder lock**, a helicopter, a quartz watch, and a sewing
  machine. Items were chosen so their explanations "did not depend on any
  specialized knowledge." Most were drawn from David Macaulay's *The Way
  Things Work 2.0* CD-ROM (Dorling Kindersley, 1996). NB: the paper's own
  reference spells the name "McCauley"; the author is David Macaulay.
- Each participant was asked about only four of the eight items, to keep the
  session under an hour.
- The five ratings, in order:
  - **T1** — initial self-rating, before any attempt to explain. Participants
    rated a list of 48 items (8 test, 40 distracter) in about 10 minutes.
  - **T2** — re-rating immediately after writing a **detailed, step-by-step
    causal explanation** of the item.
  - **T3** — re-rating after answering a **diagnostic question** requiring
    critical knowledge of the mechanism. The paper's own example for the
    cylinder lock: explain, step by step, how one could pick a cylinder lock.
    For the helicopter: explain how it changes from hovering to forward flight.
  - **T4** — re-rating of one's *prior* knowledge after reading a brief expert
    description (half a page to several pages, with diagrams).
  - **T5** — rating of one's *current* knowledge after reading that
    description; a manipulation check.
- Result: self-ratings dropped. A repeated-measures ANOVA found time
  significant, F(4, 56) = 16.195, p < .001, η² = .536. Planned linear contrasts
  for T1 vs T2, T1 vs T3 and T1 vs T4 were all significant at p < .002. T2 vs
  T3 and T2 vs T4 were not significant. T5 rose significantly against T4, T3
  and T2 (p < .003), so participants were not simply losing confidence in
  everything.
- The authors' own reading of T4: participants still judged their post-diagnostic
  ratings accurate after seeing the expert text, which rules out the objection
  that the diagnostic questions were unfair or nit-picky.

### What this source does and does not license

- It is an **empirical finding**, demonstrated across twelve studies in the one
  paper. Describe it that way, not as a theory and not as consensus.
- Study 1 is 16 graduate students at one university. Say so if you cite Study 1
  specifically. The paper's broader claims rest on the other studies too.
- The drop is a change in **self-rating**. That is self-report, not independent
  evidence of what the person could produce. This limit is the course's own
  point and should be made explicitly, as our critique, not theirs.
- A lesson that asks a reader to rate, explain and re-rate is **our adaptation**
  of their protocol, run on one person without a control. It is not a
  replication and must not be described as one.

## Pin tumbler lock — technical grounding only

**Verified by reading the article.** Wikipedia, "Pin tumbler lock":
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock>

Parts, as the article names them: an outer **casing/cylinder** (its upper
portion, holding pins and springs, is the "bible"); the rotating **plug** seated
inside it; the **keyway** at the front of the plug; a **cam** at the rear that
withdraws the bolt; **key pins** of differing lengths with rounded bottoms,
sitting in the plug; **driver pins** above them in the casing; **springs**
loading the driver pins downward. The boundary where plug and casing meet is
the **shear line**. Typically five or six pin stacks.

- **Locked:** springs push each stack down so driver pins cross from the casing
  into the plug. Pins straddle the shear line, so the plug cannot turn.
- **Unlocked:** a correctly cut key lifts each key pin so that every
  key-pin/driver-pin junction sits level with the shear line. Nothing bridges
  the boundary, the plug turns, and the cam retracts the bolt.
- **Wrong key:** its cuts lift stacks by the wrong amounts. An under-lifted
  stack leaves a driver pin in the plug; an over-lifted stack pushes a key pin
  up into the casing. Either way a pin crosses the shear line and blocks rotation.

This is **technical documentation for the worked example**. Under
`guidelines.md` §3 it grounds the subject matter and is not evidence for any
claim about understanding, learning or cognition. Do not cite it for one.

Wikipedia is an encyclopaedia, not a primary source. It is adequate for
background on a mechanism of this kind, as Week 1 used vendor and
learning-centre documentation for the same job. If you find a better-quality
technical reference, prefer it and record the swap in your handoff.
Britannica's lock article returned HTTP 403 to automated fetching and could
not be inspected, so **do not cite Britannica** on the strength of a search
snippet.
