Yes. Let's treat *Make It Stick* as something we're studying rather than mining for slogans. I'll separate **what the book argues** from **what we might eventually borrow for your course**.

Make It Stick

The book has eight main chapters. Its overall argument is roughly:

> **The strategies that make learning feel easy are often not the strategies that produce durable learning.**

That distinction between the **experience of learning** and the **actual state of learning** is especially relevant to your course.

### 1. Learning Is Misunderstood

The opening chapter establishes the problem.

People commonly rely on strategies such as rereading material and repeatedly practising the same thing. These methods can make material increasingly familiar and therefore create the *feeling* that we're mastering it.

The authors argue that this feeling is unreliable.

Imagine reading an explanation of TCP three times. On the third reading, every sentence seems obvious:

> Client sends SYN → server sends SYN-ACK → client sends ACK.

You look at it and think, *of course, I understand TCP handshakes.*

Close the source.

Now someone asks:

> Why are three messages necessary? Why wouldn't two be sufficient?

That's a different test.

The first situation tests something closer to **recognition**. The second forces you to retrieve and reason with what you learned.

The book therefore introduces an important distinction between **performance during learning** and **learning that persists afterward**.

This gives us our first connection to your course:

**Familiarity can masquerade as understanding.**

But there's already a caveat. The book is primarily concerned with **durable learning and memory**, not philosophically defining "understanding." We shouldn't turn its argument into "retrieval = understanding."

---

### 2. To Learn, Retrieve

This is probably the most important chapter for your concept.

The central idea is **retrieval practice**.

Instead of:

> read → read → read → read

the authors advocate something closer to:

> learn → remove access → attempt retrieval → discover failure → check

Testing isn't useful only for *measuring* learning.

**Trying to retrieve something changes learning itself.**

For example, suppose you've just learned why seasons occur.

Instead of rereading the explanation five times, close it and answer:

> Why is Australia experiencing summer while Europe experiences winter?

You might discover:

> "It's because Australia is... closer to the Sun?"

Now you've exposed a misconception.

That failure is informative.

You return to the source and discover that Earth's axial tilt, rather than simply distance from the Sun, explains the seasonal pattern.

This is extremely close to the exercise you've independently been describing:

> **Learn → remove source → reconstruct → inspect gaps.**

But there's a subtle difference worth preserving.

Your reconstruction exercise potentially asks for something **more demanding** than simple retrieval. You're interested in reconstructing relationships, mechanisms and explanations—not merely recalling facts.

That may eventually be where your course departs from the book.

---

### 3. Mix Up Your Practice

Now the authors complicate practice itself.

Three ideas become important:

**Spacing** — revisit something after time has passed rather than massing practice together.

**Interleaving** — mix different kinds of problems rather than completing one type repeatedly.

**Variation** — practise knowledge under different conditions.

Consider learning three statistical methods.

The comfortable version is:

> 20 t-test problems
> then 20 ANOVA problems
> then 20 regression problems.

Eventually the student doesn't really need to decide **which technique applies**. The worksheet structure tells them.

Interleave them:

> regression → t-test → ANOVA → regression → ANOVA...

Now each problem introduces another question:

> **What kind of problem is this?**

The task becomes harder, and performance during practice may actually look worse.

But that difficulty can support better discrimination and later application.

This introduces a fascinating recurring principle:

> **Feeling worse at learning can sometimes accompany better learning.**

That attacks another assumption we often make:

> Easy learning experience = effective learning.

Not necessarily.

---

### 4. Embrace Difficulties

This chapter develops what researchers call **desirable difficulties**.

Some obstacles make learning more effortful in ways that improve later retention or transfer.

For example, retrieving something after you've partially forgotten it can require more effort than immediately repeating it.

That effort can be productive.

But the word **desirable** matters enormously.

Difficulty by itself isn't educational.

Giving students an unreadable textbook doesn't magically improve learning. Neither does confusing instruction or impossible assignments.

The useful difficulty must require the learner to perform productive cognitive work.

This could eventually become important for your course design because your "remove the source" rule deliberately introduces difficulty.

Instead of allowing:

> "I'll replay the video until I can repeat what it said."

you impose:

> "The source is gone. Build the explanation yourself."

The difficulty isn't there to punish the student. It's there to make otherwise invisible gaps visible.

---

### 5. Avoid Illusions of Knowing

For **your course**, this might actually be the conceptual centre of *Make It Stick*.

Humans aren't particularly reliable judges of their own knowledge.

Familiarity creates one problem.

If you've seen something repeatedly, processing it becomes easier. That ease can be interpreted as:

> "I know this."

But what you may actually know is:

> "I recognise this."

Imagine looking at a map of Australia every day.

You become extremely familiar with it.

Then someone removes the map and asks you to draw the states and territories accurately.

Suddenly familiarity and production separate.

This introduces **metacognition**: our ability to monitor and judge our own thinking and learning.

And those judgments can be wrong.

This connects extremely strongly to the question you asked earlier:

> *If I think I understand something but actually don't, how could I know?*

The book's answer is essentially:

**Don't rely solely on the feeling of knowing. Create situations where knowledge has to perform.**

Retrieve it.

Explain it.

Apply it.

Test it.

Seek feedback.

Calibration improves when predictions about what we know encounter evidence about what we can actually do.

That is much more powerful than simply telling students:

> "Be aware of what you don't know."

---

### 6. Get Beyond Learning Styles

The book challenges the popular idea that students learn better when instruction is matched to supposed categories such as:

> visual learner
> auditory learner
> kinesthetic learner.

The authors argue that evidence doesn't support the common "matching" hypothesis in the way it is popularly presented.

Instead, they emphasise abilities, prior knowledge, strategies and adapting to the nature of the material.

There's an important philosophical implication for your course:

**Learning isn't necessarily about finding the information format that feels most comfortable.**

Sometimes the structure of the thing being learned should determine how we represent it.

You don't understand geometry merely by discovering your preferred medium for geometry.

You need to engage with the relationships geometry actually contains.

That pushes against a consumer model of education:

> "Give me information in the format I like."

toward:

> "What intellectual operation does this material require me to perform?"

---

### 7. Increase Your Abilities

This chapter moves toward beliefs about intellectual ability and how capabilities can develop through practice.

The book discusses ideas around brain plasticity, effort, persistence and what were then influential discussions of growth mindset.

For our purposes, I wouldn't make this chapter central to your SlopU course.

The useful connection is simpler:

**Learning changes what you are capable of doing; knowledge isn't merely information stored somewhere.**

But if we imported everything here—mindset, intelligence, brain plasticity, etc.—the course could easily drift into generic "become a better learner" territory.

I'd treat this as supporting material rather than the spine.

---

### 8. Make It Stick

The final chapter turns the research into practical recommendations for learners, teachers and trainers.

Many previous ideas converge:

> retrieval
> spacing
> interleaving
> generation
> reflection
> elaboration
> calibration

Rather than repeatedly exposing yourself to information, effective learning requires **doing something with knowledge**.

For example, after a lecture:

Don't merely reread the slides.

Ask:

> What were the central ideas?

> How do they relate?

> Can I explain them without looking?

> What examples can I generate?

> Where does this connect to something I already know?

> Which part can I not reconstruct?

Then return to the material and compare.

That last comparison is especially important for your idea.

---

# So what does *Make It Stick* actually give your course?

If we compress the whole book, I see a chain like this:

**Exposure**

↓

**Familiarity**

↓

*"This makes sense."*

But that feeling can be misleading.

So:

**Remove support**

↓

**Retrieve**

↓

**Attempt**

↓

**Failure becomes visible**

↓

**Feedback**

↓

**Correct/reconstruct**

↓

**Retrieve again later**

↓

**More durable learning**

Your course idea seems to start inside that chain and then ask a question *Make It Stick* doesn't fully answer:

> **When does successful retrieval become understanding?**

Suppose I can perfectly retrieve:

> "Backpropagation computes gradients using the chain rule."

Have I understood backpropagation?

Maybe not.

Suppose I can explain the mechanism.

Better.

Suppose I can explain **why** gradient information must propagate backward.

Better again.

Suppose I can predict what happens if a component changes.

Stronger evidence.

Suppose I can identify where my explanation stops working.

Stronger still.

And suppose I can reconstruct the idea from first principles when my original explanation is taken away.

Now we're approaching the territory you've been interested in.

So I would extract **three foundational propositions** from *Make It Stick* for the SlopU course:

> **1. Feeling that you know is not reliable evidence that you know.**

> **2. Retrieval isn't merely an assessment of learning; retrieval can produce learning.**

> **3. Difficulty can expose and strengthen knowledge when the difficulty requires productive cognitive work.**

But I would **not** extract:

> "Retrieval proves understanding."

The book doesn't give us enough to make that leap.

And that unresolved gap is useful. It gives us somewhere to go next: **What exactly counts as understanding beyond remembering?**

Before moving to *The Craft of Research*, I'd actually spend some time on that question. It may determine whether your SlopU course becomes an intellectually coherent course about **reconstructing understanding**, or merely a polished course about effective study techniques.
===
Now the second book changes the question in a useful way.

The Craft of Research

Where *Make It Stick* roughly asks:

> **How can learning become durable rather than merely familiar?**

*The Craft of Research* asks something closer to:

> **How do you turn what you think you know into a claim that another person has reason to accept?**

That makes it useful for your course because it moves us from **private understanding → externally defensible understanding**.

I'll follow the book's broad progression rather than treating it as a list of research-writing tips.

### 1. Thinking in Print / Becoming a Researcher

The book starts by reframing what research actually is.

A beginner can think research means:

> choose topic → collect information → write report.

But the authors argue that research is fundamentally about entering a **conversation with readers**.

You don't merely report what you've discovered. You anticipate another person's questions:

> Why should I care?
> How do you know?
> What evidence supports that?
> Why does that evidence support this conclusion?
> What about this alternative explanation?

This distinction is important.

Imagine a student researches recommendation algorithms and writes:

> "Recommendation systems use user behaviour to decide what content people are likely to engage with."

That's information.

A research problem starts appearing when the student asks something like:

> "How does optimising recommendations for engagement change the kinds of information users repeatedly encounter?"

Now there is something to investigate.

For your course, this introduces a major progression from *Make It Stick*:

**Remembering information is not enough. Even explaining information isn't necessarily enough. You need to interrogate what you think you know.**

---

### 2. Connecting With Your Reader

Research is communication between a researcher and a reader.

That sounds obvious, but the book treats the reader as an active intellectual participant.

The writer needs to understand:

> What does my reader already know?

> What do they expect?

> What will they question?

> Why should this problem matter to them?

This immediately complicates your educational-video idea.

Suppose the assignment says:

> "Explain gradient descent."

A student could produce a technically correct explanation.

But *The Craft of Research* pushes another question:

> **Explain it to whom?**

An explanation for a machine-learning researcher is different from one for a first-year student.

Therefore explanation isn't simply:

**knowledge → words**

It's closer to:

**your model → reader's existing model → carefully chosen bridge between them.**

That's something we didn't get strongly from *Make It Stick*.

---

### 3. From Topics to Questions

This is one of the most useful sections for your course.

Students often begin with **topics**:

> AI
> climate change
> photography
> Roman architecture
> computer networks.

But a topic isn't yet a research question.

The book encourages moving through something roughly like:

> **Topic → Question → Significance**

For example:

**Topic**

> Wikipedia.

**Question**

> How does Wikipedia resolve disagreements between editors?

Then:

> Why does that matter?

Perhaps:

> Because Wikipedia presents a relatively stable article to readers even though the knowledge behind it may be actively contested.

Now we're no longer merely "learning about Wikipedia."

We're investigating something.

This is useful for your SlopU course because **curiosity needs direction**.

Your earlier concern was that today's information environment gives us practically unlimited paths:

> video → link → Reddit → paper → another video → Wikipedia → podcast → AI explanation...

You can consume indefinitely.

A **question** constrains that search.

Instead of:

> "I want to learn about neural networks."

you have:

> "Why does changing the learning rate sometimes prevent a neural network from converging?"

Now information has a purpose.

That's potentially an entire week of your course:

**A topic gives you territory. A question gives you direction.**

---

### 4. From Questions to Problems

The book then distinguishes having a question from having a **problem worth investigating**.

You might ask:

> "When was the first neural network created?"

That's answerable.

But answering it doesn't necessarily change much.

A stronger research problem connects the question to consequences:

> "Why did early neural-network research repeatedly lose and regain scientific attention?"

Now understanding the history might help explain something larger about scientific expectations, computational limitations, funding, or changing technical capabilities.

The book frequently pushes researchers toward the reader's implicit response:

> **So what?**

Not in a dismissive way.

It's a test of significance.

This could protect your course from another form of information consumption:

> collecting interesting facts without developing an intellectual reason for collecting them.

---

### 5. From Problems to Sources

Now sources enter.

And there's an important connection to your original course idea.

A source isn't something you simply **consume**.

It has a role in an investigation.

You ask:

> What does this source claim?

> What evidence does it provide?

> What does it assume?

> How does it relate to other sources?

> Where do sources disagree?

> What can this source actually support?

This changes reading.

Instead of:

> "I read five papers about X."

the researcher should eventually be able to say:

> "Paper A provides evidence for this part of my argument, but assumes X. Paper B challenges that assumption using Y. Paper C addresses a different population, so I can't use it to support the same claim."

That's a qualitatively different relationship with information.

And I think this connects beautifully to your concern about **information saturation**.

The solution isn't necessarily:

> consume less information.

It's:

> **give information a job.**

Once you're pursuing a question, some information becomes relevant, some peripheral, some contradictory, and some useless.

---

### 6. Engaging Sources

Here's where we can connect back to your reconstruction idea.

A weak learner treats the source as authority:

> Source says X → I write X.

A stronger researcher asks:

> Why does the source conclude X?

And perhaps:

> Could the same evidence support Y?

> What assumptions connect the evidence to X?

> Does another source disagree?

> What happens if I remove this source from my argument?

That last question is particularly interesting for your course.

Remember your earlier exercise:

> **Learn something → remove source → reconstruct → audit gaps.**

We can now extend it:

> **Read source → remove source → reconstruct its argument → reopen source → compare your reconstruction with what it actually argued.**

That's much stronger than merely recalling its contents.

---

### 7. Making Good Arguments

This is perhaps the book's biggest contribution to your course.

An argument isn't merely a claim.

Very roughly, you need things such as:

**Claim** — what you want the reader to accept.

**Reasons** — why you think the claim is true.

**Evidence** — what supports those reasons.

**Warrant** — why the evidence/reason actually connects to the claim.

Plus acknowledgement of alternatives, limitations, objections and qualifications.

Consider:

> **Claim:** Doomscrolling reduces understanding.

Sounds plausible.

Then:

> **Evidence:** People who spend more time consuming short-form content perform worse on some measure.

Even if that evidence were real, we aren't finished.

A reader can ask:

> Why does that establish that doomscrolling *caused* the difference?

Perhaps people who already struggle with sustained attention consume more short-form media.

Or perhaps another variable affects both.

This is where **warrants and alternative explanations** matter.

For your course, this introduces another level:

> **Can you defend the relationships inside your explanation?**

Not merely:

> "Can you remember the explanation?"

---

### 8. Claims

A good claim must be appropriately specific and appropriately qualified.

Compare:

> "Social media destroys attention."

with:

> "Frequent task-switching between short-form videos may reduce performance on tasks requiring sustained attention under these conditions..."

The second isn't weaker simply because it's less dramatic.

Its boundaries are clearer.

Understanding something partly involves knowing **how far your claim travels**.

This is extremely relevant to your idea of identifying where understanding fails.

Someone who poorly understands something often says:

> "X always causes Y."

Someone with deeper understanding might say:

> "X produces Y under A and B, but not necessarily under C, and we don't have enough evidence about D."

That leads toward something important for your course:

**Understanding includes knowing the boundaries of your model.**

---

### 9. Reasons and Evidence

The book distinguishes what you *think* from what you can **show**.

Imagine a student says:

> "Students learn better from educational videos."

Why?

> "Because videos are engaging."

That's a reason.

But now:

> How do you know they're engaging?

And more importantly:

> Why would engagement necessarily imply learning?

We have to keep descending through the argument.

This is potentially powerful for your course.

Students could literally perform an **argument audit**:

> Claim
> ↓
> Why?
> ↓
> Reason
> ↓
> How do you know?
> ↓
> Evidence
> ↓
> Why does that evidence justify the reason?
> ↓
> Warrant

Eventually something breaks.

And **where it breaks reveals something the student doesn't understand or cannot justify.**

That's very close to the intellectual experience you've been trying to create.

---

### 10. Warrants

Warrants are particularly interesting because they're often invisible.

Suppose I say:

> "Alex has watched 40 hours of calculus lectures, therefore Alex understands calculus."

Claim:

> Alex understands calculus.

Evidence:

> Alex watched 40 hours of lectures.

Hidden warrant:

> Spending substantial time consuming instruction produces understanding.

But that's exactly the assumption your course questions.

Once we expose the warrant, we can challenge it.

That's why warrants matter: they reveal the **hidden bridge** between evidence and conclusion.

Your course could use this beyond academic research.

Whenever students encounter:

> "X, therefore Y."

ask:

> **What must be true for that 'therefore' to work?**

That's an excellent reconstruction exercise.

---

### 11. Acknowledgements and Responses

Good arguments anticipate objections.

Instead of hiding evidence that complicates your explanation:

> "This proves X."

you might say:

> "This supports X under these conditions, although Y provides a plausible alternative explanation..."

This isn't weakness.

It's evidence that you understand the landscape surrounding your claim.

And that suggests another possible definition for your course:

> **Understanding isn't only knowing why your explanation works. It's knowing what could make it wrong.**

That takes us significantly beyond *Make It Stick*.

---

### 12. Planning, Drafting and Revising

The later sections turn toward producing research writing: organising arguments, drafting, revising, introductions, conclusions and communicating evidence clearly.

For your SlopU course, I wouldn't import most of this directly.

Otherwise your course slowly turns into:

**Academic Writing 101.**

That's not your central idea.

What matters is the deeper principle underneath revision:

> **Externalising thought makes it inspectable.**

An idea inside your head can feel coherent.

Write it down.

Draw it.

Explain it.

Make the video.

Construct the argument.

Suddenly contradictions become visible.

This connects very strongly to reconstruction.

---

# Now combine Books 1 and 2

This is where things become much more interesting.

***Make It Stick*** gives us:

> Don't trust the feeling of knowing.

So we remove support.

> **Can you retrieve it?**

But ***The Craft of Research*** introduces another challenge:

> Retrieval still isn't sufficient.

You retrieved:

> "The Industrial Revolution began in Britain because Britain had coal, capital, labour and technological innovation."

Okay.

Now I ask:

> Why those factors?

> What evidence establishes their importance?

> Why Britain rather than another country with some of those characteristics?

> Do historians agree?

> What alternative explanations exist?

> How confident should we be?

Now we're probing something different.

So I think we can construct a ladder:

**1. Recognition**

> I've seen this.

↓

**2. Recall**

> I can retrieve this without the source.

↓

**3. Reconstruction**

> I can rebuild the relationships between the ideas.

↓

**4. Explanation**

> I can make that model intelligible to another person.

↓

**5. Justification**

> I can support the claims inside my explanation.

↓

**6. Challenge**

> I can respond when someone attacks the assumptions, evidence or relationships.

↓

**7. Boundary**

> I know where my explanation stops working.

That is much closer to a **theory of understanding**.

And importantly, neither book alone gives us this entire ladder.

# There's also a problem we should preserve

This is where I'd challenge our emerging model.

Imagine I memorize an argument generated by AI.

I can:

* recall it,
* explain it,
* cite its evidence,
* state its warrants,
* mention objections,
* list its limitations.

Do I understand it?

**Maybe. Maybe not.**

We've still got something missing.

For example, could I handle a situation I haven't seen before?

Could I use the concept to predict something?

Could I notice when the concept is being misapplied?

Could I construct a new example?

Could I connect it to another concept without being told the connection?

Could I repair my model after discovering contradictory evidence?

Those questions move us toward **transfer, application and model-building**.

And that is why I don't think we should start writing your 12 weeks yet.

After *Make It Stick* and *The Craft of Research*, we have two pieces:

> **Book 1: Don't confuse familiarity with learning.**

> **Book 2: Don't confuse having an explanation with having a justified explanation.**

The third body of material should help us investigate the remaining gap:

> **Don't confuse being able to repeat/reconstruct an explanation with being able to use the underlying model.**

That's where I'd continue next—and it may lead us beyond *Learning How to Learn* into work specifically about **understanding, transfer, explanation, and mental models**, because I'm increasingly suspicious that *Learning How to Learn* alone won't give your course the third intellectual pillar it needs.
