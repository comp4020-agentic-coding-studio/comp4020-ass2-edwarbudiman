Both are promising because they force students to **reconstruct**, not merely consume. But as assessments, they are still missing a few things that would make them defensible at university level.

For **“Create me a video of a concept that you learn”**, the biggest problem is that a polished video can hide shallow understanding. A student can script from sources, imitate a YouTube explainer, or use strong editing and still not actually understand the concept deeply.

What is missing is evidence of **independent reconstruction and testing**. The assignment should require the student to show how their explanation changed after discovering gaps.

A stronger version would be:

> Create a short explainer video teaching an unfamiliar concept in your own model. Before producing the final video, submit an initial no-source reconstruction, identify at least two gaps or errors after checking sources, and show how those discoveries changed the final explanation.

Now the marker is not only grading the video. They can see the learning process behind it.

For **“Create an explanation from back history, then how in this modern era it developed”**, there is another risk: it can easily become a normal research essay.

A student could just produce:

> “First X happened, then Y happened, then today we have Z.”

That demonstrates information gathering, but not necessarily understanding.

The missing piece is a **causal question**.

Instead of asking for history for its own sake, ask students to explain:

> **Why did this concept become what it is today?**

For example, rather than:

> “Explain the history of machine learning and modern AI.”

you might require:

> “Choose one modern concept or system. Reconstruct the chain of problems, constraints, and conceptual changes that made its current form possible. For each major transition, explain what the previous approach could not do and what the new development changed.”

Now chronology is not enough.

The student has to understand relationships:

> old model → limitation → new idea → consequence

That is much stronger.

The deeper issue with both assessments is that right now they assess mostly the **final artefact**.

Your course seems to care about something else:

> how someone moves from exposure to genuine understanding.

So each assessment probably needs three layers:

1. **Reconstruction** — what can you explain without leaning on the source?
2. **Verification** — where was your model wrong, incomplete, or oversimplified?
3. **Revision** — how did checking and testing change your explanation?

Then the artefact can differ.

One assessment could culminate in a video.

Another could culminate in a historical explanation.

But underneath, they both exercise the same course method.

That coherence matters a lot.

For example:

**Assessment 1 — Explain It Without the Explainer**

Choose an unfamiliar concept. After initial study, close your sources and build an explanation from memory. Test that explanation against authoritative sources, identify the important gaps, and produce a 5-minute explainer video showing the corrected model.

This tests:

> Can you reconstruct and repair understanding?

Then:

**Assessment 2 — How Did We Get Here?**

Choose a contemporary concept, technology, practice, or system. Trace 3–5 important conceptual transitions in its development. At each transition, explain the problem the previous model could not solve, what changed, and why that change mattered. Finish by explaining which historical assumptions still shape the modern version.

This tests:

> Can you understand a concept deeply enough to reconstruct why its present form exists?

Those two now feel related rather than arbitrary.

And I would challenge one more thing: **do not let students choose literally anything** unless you design strong constraints. Total freedom sounds attractive, but it can make marking inconsistent and encourage students to choose topics they already know.

A better constraint might be:

> Students may choose any topic, but it must be unfamiliar to them, narrow enough to reconstruct within the assignment, supported by credible sources, and capable of being tested through explanation, prediction, application, or comparison.

That keeps the “art-like freedom” you value without making the assessment shapeless.

The strongest missing piece, though, is probably this:

> **Where does the student demonstrate that their understanding changed?**

If you can make that visible in both assessments, you have something distinctive. Without it, the first risks becoming “make an educational video,” and the second risks becoming “write a history report.”

With it, both become assessments of your central course idea:

**Understanding is demonstrated by reconstruction, challenged through testing, and deepened through revision.**

Yes — **making an educational video can absolutely be evidence of learning**. The issue is not that the format is weak; it is that the assignment needs to make the learning visible enough that the marker can distinguish “I assembled a good video” from “I developed a good understanding.”

A student might learn a concept, script it, simplify it, choose examples, and explain it in their own words. That process can be very powerful because teaching something forces decisions about what is essential, what can be omitted, and how ideas connect. In that sense, the video is not just presentation; it can be a **reconstruction artefact**.

The devil’s-advocate question is: how does the professor know the student actually did that work of understanding, rather than mostly paraphrasing a source or reproducing an existing explainer? You do not need a huge process journal to solve this. You could simply require the video to include one small reflective element such as:

> “The part I initially misunderstood was…”
> “I first thought X, but after working through Y I changed my explanation to…”
> “The hardest part to explain accurately was…”

That might be enough. The course does not need to become bureaucratic.

Your second point is even more important: **you should not design the course around computer science simply because that is the domain you personally know best.**

Instead, design the assessment around a structure that works across domains.

For example, rather than asking students to trace “technological development,” ask:

> **Choose something that exists in its present form because earlier ideas, problems, discoveries, practices, or constraints changed over time. Explain how it became what it is now.**

That works for computing, but also many other fields.

A computing student could explain:

> command-line interfaces → graphical interfaces → touch interfaces

A music student could explain:

> acoustic recording → magnetic tape → digital recording → streaming

A biology student could explain:

> early inheritance theories → Mendelian genetics → chromosomes → molecular genetics

An architecture student could explain:

> load-bearing masonry → steel frames → reinforced concrete → modern high-rise construction

A linguistics student could explain:

> prescriptive grammar traditions → structural linguistics → generative grammar → contemporary approaches

A fashion student could investigate how a garment, material, manufacturing technique, or aesthetic changed.

So the invariant is not **“computer history.”**

It is:

> **Can you reconstruct why the present looks the way it does?**

That is a very transferable intellectual skill.

I would therefore separate your two assessment ideas by the kind of understanding they test.

The **educational video** asks:

> **Can you take something you have learned and reconstruct it clearly enough for someone else to understand?**

The **development/history explanation** asks:

> **Can you understand something not just as it exists now, but as the result of earlier problems, decisions, constraints, and changes?**

Those are actually quite different and complementary.

The first is about **structural understanding**: “How does this concept fit together?”

The second is about **developmental understanding**: “Why did it become this way?”

And you could have a third later assessment about **application**:

> **Can you use your understanding somewhere it was not originally shown to you?**

That would give the course a very coherent progression:

> **Explain it → explain how it became → use it somewhere new.**

That is starting to feel stronger than forcing every assessment to explicitly show “I was wrong, then I repaired myself.” The repair idea can still exist inside the pedagogy, but it does not have to dominate every assignment.

The key is that each assessment asks students to **produce evidence of understanding**, while still allowing them to choose subjects from very different backgrounds.
