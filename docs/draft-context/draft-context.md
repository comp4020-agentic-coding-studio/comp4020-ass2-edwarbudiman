Exactly. The way to make this feel like a **university course rather than “better studying tips”** is to stop teaching habits directly and instead make the course investigate a phenomenon:

> **Why does information consumption so often create the feeling of understanding before understanding actually exists?**

Then “Consumption produces familiarity. Reconstruction produces understanding.” becomes not a slogan, but the **course thesis that students repeatedly test**.

A university-worthy version needs three things: an object of study, a method, and something students progressively build. For this course, the object of study is **the gap between familiarity and understanding**. The method is **reconstruction**. The thing students build is **their own increasingly defensible model of something they chose to learn**.

That distinction matters. A productivity course says, “Use active recall.” Your course should ask, “What can a learner do after an explanation that demonstrates they possess its structure rather than merely recognize it?” Active recall might appear, but only as one possible experiment.

Likewise, instead of saying “avoid doomscrolling,” Week 1 might study a stranger question: **What does unlimited access to explanations do to our sense of progress?** Students could spend twenty minutes following a recommendation trail on a topic, then try to draw what they actually learned without reopening anything. Suddenly doomscrolling becomes evidence in an investigation about knowledge, not a wellness lecture.

I think the strongest course structure is to give every student **one learning object** for the semester. Not necessarily the same subject. One student might choose “how JPEG compression works,” another “how espresso extraction works,” another “how parliamentary voting works,” another “how a synthesiser creates a sound.”

They keep returning to that object as the course becomes more demanding.

Early on, they can describe what they have consumed.

Then they must map it.

Then explain it without the source.

Then identify what their explanation cannot account for.

Then predict something.

Then change a variable.

Then compare conflicting explanations.

Eventually they must **reconstruct a working version, demonstration, argument, model, or procedure from what they claim to understand**.

That gives the semester a concrete transformation:

**“I have read about this.”**
→ **“I recognise the terminology.”**
→ **“I can explain the mechanism.”**
→ **“I know where my explanation breaks.”**
→ **“I can use the model to predict.”**
→ **“I can reconstruct the thing.”**

Now you have an epistemology course disguised as something approachable.

That is much more substantial than generic learning advice.

There is another important decision I would make: **don't present reconstruction as automatically superior to consumption.**

That would become another simplistic self-help claim.

Consumption is necessary. You cannot reconstruct quantum mechanics from pure determination. Experts, books, demonstrations, videos and AI provide scaffolding that would otherwise take years to rediscover.

So the intellectually interesting question is:

> **What should consumption hand over to reconstruction, and when?**

That tension can run throughout the course.

Sometimes the right action is reading another explanation.

Sometimes it is closing every tab and trying.

Sometimes you discover during reconstruction that you're missing prerequisite knowledge, so you return to consumption.

That gives you a loop rather than a slogan:

**Encounter → Orient → Model → Attempt → Fail → Seek → Revise → Reconstruct**

I actually think that loop could become the visual identity of the course website. Each week changes which part of the loop students are capable of performing.

And that suggests a more distinctive course premise:

> **This course studies the moment when learning stops being about receiving information and starts being about making something survive without its source.**

“Survive without its source” is interesting because it gives you a practical test.

Can your understanding survive when the YouTube video is closed?

Can it survive a new example?

Can it survive disagreement?

Can it survive forgetting an equation?

Can it survive a changed condition?

Can it survive having to build the thing yourself?

Those are much better course questions than “How should I study?”

### Where I would push the SlopU personality

I wouldn't make SlopU's course title too academic.

The course itself can be intellectually serious while the packaging has a little bite.

Some titles that fit the concept better now:

**SLOPxxxx — You Watched the Video. Now What?**
*From familiarity to reconstruction*

This might be my favourite SlopU title because the joke communicates the problem instantly.

A marker understands the course before reading a paragraph.

Another strong one:

**SLOPxxxx — I Get It, I Think**
*The gap between explanation and understanding*

That title captures the false-confidence phenomenon extremely well.

More serious:

**SLOPxxxx — After the Explanation**
*How understanding survives its source*

Or a more provocative one:

**SLOPxxxx — Close the Tab**
*Learning beyond information consumption*

“Close the Tab” could actually become a recurring course ritual. Certain activities literally begin with:

> Close the book.
> Close the video.
> Close the AI chat.
> Now tell us what remains.

That is specific enough to become part of the course's voice and perhaps even a `CLAUDE.md` rule later: activities should expose the difference between supported performance and unsupported understanding.

### The biggest thing I would avoid

Don't make each week a “learning technique.”

For example, I would avoid:

**Week 1:** Doomscrolling
**Week 2:** Goal setting
**Week 3:** Note taking
**Week 4:** Active recall
**Week 5:** Feynman technique
**Week 6:** Spaced repetition

Even though all those things could be relevant, that structure kills your thesis. It becomes a Study Skills 101 syllabus.

Instead, the weeks should describe **problems in the journey from consumption to reconstruction**.

Something much closer to:

**1 — Too Much to Know**
What information abundance changes about learning.

**2 — Choosing a Question**
Turning curiosity into something that can actually be investigated.

**3 — The Feeling of Progress**
Why exposure, fluency and recognition impersonate understanding.

**4 — What Is the Model?**
Moving from facts to relationships and mechanisms.

**5 — Explain It Without Them**
What disappears when the source disappears.

**6 — The Missing Step**
Finding invisible assumptions and prerequisites.

**7 — Break Your Explanation**
Using counterexamples and changed conditions.

**8 — Ask Another Source**
What disagreement reveals about your model.

**9 — Predict Before Looking**
Using understanding to generate expectations.

**10 — Build a Bad Version**
Early reconstruction as a diagnostic tool.

**11 — Reconstruct**
Producing something that functions without imitation.

**12 — What Survived?**
Showing how the learner's model changed across the semester.

Those are still provisional, but notice the difference: there is a **narrative**.

Week 10 makes no sense without Weeks 4–9.

Week 3 destabilises how students think about learning. Week 4 gives them a replacement. Weeks 5–9 progressively stress-test it. Weeks 10–12 force it into the world.

That is what makes it feel like a course.

And I think we now have a useful criterion for every idea we add:

> **Does this help us investigate the gap between familiarity and understanding, or is it merely good study advice?**

If it's merely a useful habit, it probably doesn't deserve a week.

If it reveals something about that gap, it belongs.

My strongest direction at this point would be to temporarily call the course **“I Get It, I Think”** while we design it, and treat **“Consumption produces familiarity. Reconstruction produces understanding.”** as the thesis we are trying to complicate—not merely prove.

The next useful step is probably **not** writing all twelve weeks. It would be defining precisely what we mean by **“understanding”** and **“reconstruction”**, because if those remain vague, the whole course will eventually drift back into productivity advice.
